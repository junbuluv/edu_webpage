## The fundamental identity

In the simplified national-income framework used here, what a country
saves and does not invest at home becomes net acquisition of foreign
assets. Abstracting from international income and transfers, the current
account is net exports, so:

$$
S - I = NX.
$$

If the US saves more than it invests, $S - I > 0$, so $NX > 0$: the US
runs a trade surplus and accumulates foreign assets. If the US invests
more than it saves, $NX < 0$: a trade deficit financed by foreigners
acquiring domestic debt, equity, real estate, or other assets—not only by
bank lending. In fuller balance-of-payments accounts, $S-I$ maps to the
current account, which also includes net income and transfers.

## The real exchange rate is the price that clears trade

The real exchange rate $\varepsilon$ is how many units of foreign goods
trade for one unit of US goods. A higher $\varepsilon$ (a stronger dollar
in real terms) makes US exports more expensive and imports cheaper,
reducing $NX$.

The simulation uses a positive-domain trade schedule:

$$
NX(\varepsilon) = \overline{NX} - b\ln(\varepsilon),
\qquad b > 0,\; \varepsilon > 0.
$$

Its loanable-funds side is explicit as well (all flow amounts are annual
USD billions, and $r^*$ is entered in percentage points):

$$
S(r^*,m_S)=1500m_S+20r^*,
\qquad
I(r^*)=1700-40r^*.
$$

Thus the world-rate slider raises saving by \$20B and lowers investment by
\$40B per percentage point; the saving-multiplier slider changes the
intercept. The calibrated trade schedule starts from
$\overline{NX}=200$ billion USD and $b=700$ billion USD.

Equilibrium $\varepsilon^*$ is whatever value makes $NX(\varepsilon) =
S - I$. If saving rises, the right-hand side rises, so $NX$ must rise,
which requires $\varepsilon$ to fall — a weaker real dollar.

## Tariff neutrality in the benchmark model

Try sliding the **tariff** lever. In this experiment the tariff shifts
$NX(\varepsilon)$ upward while **holding $S$ and $I$ fixed**. Equilibrium
$NX$ therefore remains $S-I$, and $\varepsilon$ rises until the stronger
real dollar offsets the initial trade-schedule shift. This is a
trade-balance-neutrality result, not a claim about who bears the tariff.

Outside the benchmark, tariffs can change government saving, investment,
income, expectations, pass-through, and foreign retaliation. Any of those
channels can move $S-I$ or reshape the trade schedule, so neither a fixed
trade balance nor complete consumer pass-through is a universal empirical
prediction.
