## The equation first

$$
P_{\text{callable}} = P_{\text{straight}} - C_{\text{call}}.
$$

A callable bond is worth the same bond without the call feature
(the **straight bond**, priced as in
[Bond Pricing and Yield to Maturity](/lessons/fin-3610/bond-pricing-and-yield)),
minus the value of a call option that the issuer holds. The rest of
this lesson explains each term and what the minus sign does to the
price.

## What the contract says

A **callable bond** gives the issuer the right to repay the bond
before maturity. The bond contract fixes the terms:

- **Call price.** What the issuer pays to retire the bond early,
  usually face value plus a premium. Prices in this lesson are quoted
  per 100 of face value, so a call price of 102 means \$1,020 for a
  \$1,000 bond.
- **Call protection period.** The years after issue during which the
  issuer may not call. It ends on the **first call date**. A 10-year
  bond first callable in year 3 has three years of call protection.

## Who holds the option

In [Financial Options](/lessons/fin-3610/financial-options), a call
option is the right to buy an asset at a fixed strike price. Here the
asset is the bond, the strike is the call price, and the right
belongs to the **issuer**. The investor is on the other side: the
investor has written the call. Unlike the European calls in that
lesson, the issuer may use this one on any call date from the first
call date on.

Buying a callable bond is therefore two positions at once:

| Position                  | What it is                                   | Value to the investor  |
| ------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- | ---------------------- |
| Long a straight bond      | The coupons and the face value               | $+P_{\text{straight}}$ |
| Short a call on that bond | The issuer may buy it back at the call price | $-C_{\text{call}}$     |

When does the issuer use the option? When rates fall. Suppose a firm
issued at 6% and can now borrow at 4%. The old bond's remaining
payments, discounted at 4%, are worth more than the call price. The
issuer calls, pays the call price, and borrows again at 4%. A
homeowner who refinances a mortgage after rates drop makes the same
decision.

The timing is bad for the investor. The money comes back exactly when
it can only be reinvested at lower rates.

The chart prices both bonds on a binomial tree. It is the same idea as
the binomial option model in the Financial Options lesson, with one
step per year and the one-year interest rate in place of the stock
price. Each year the rate moves up or down with equal odds. With a 5%
yield and 15% volatility, next year's one-year rate is either 4.26% or
5.75%. Working back from maturity, in every year from the first call
date on the issuer compares the value of leaving the bond outstanding
with the call price, and calls when the call price is lower.

Three things to try:

1. **Drag the market yield from 12% down to 1%.** At high yields the
   two curves are nearly the same line, because nobody refinances a 6%
   bond when new debt costs 10%. They separate as the yield comes down
   toward the coupon rate, and the gap widens fast below it.
2. **Set the yield to 6% and move rate volatility between 0% and
   15%.** At 0% the option is worth nothing: the bond is worth 100,
   calling it at 102 would cost the issuer 2 points, and rates will
   never change. At 15% it is worth 2.54, because rates might fall
   later.
3. **Move the first call date to year 1.** The callable curve drops to
   within one coupon of the dashed call-price line. With the call one
   year away, the bond can be worth at most one coupon plus the call
   price, discounted one year.

## The price ceiling and negative convexity

Look at the callable curve to the left of the coupon rate. The
straight bond keeps climbing as yields fall. The callable bond climbs
far more slowly, because the lower yields go, the more certain the
call. From a 5% yield down to 1%, the baseline straight bond gains
about 40 points and the callable bond about 14.

Take the extreme case where a call on the first call date is certain.
The investor then receives the coupons up to that date and the call
price $K$ on it. With $n$ years to the first call date, the present
value of those payments is the **price to call**:

$$
P_{\text{to call}} = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{c \cdot F}{(1+y)^t} + \frac{K}{(1+y)^n}.
$$

A callable bond is never worth more than its price to call, and never
worth more than the straight bond. For a bond that can be called right
now, $n = 0$ and the price to call is the call price itself, so the
call price is a ceiling on the bond's price (setting aside the notice
the issuer must give before a call). During call protection the
ceiling sits higher, because the above-market coupons are safe until
the first call date. In the baseline at a 1% yield, the straight bond
is worth 147.36 and the callable bond 116.65, exactly its price to
call. Move the first call date to year 1 and it is worth only 106.93.

The ceiling changes the shape of the curve. Start the baseline bond at
a 5% yield and move the yield one point each way:

| Yield moves from 5% to | Straight bond | Callable bond |
| ---------------------- | ------------- | ------------- |
| 4%                     | +8.50         | +4.35         |
| 6%                     | −7.72         | −5.09         |

The straight bond gains more than it loses. That is the convexity
from the bond pricing lesson, and it favors the investor. The callable
bond gains less than it loses. Its curve bends the other way, which is
called **negative convexity**. The investor keeps about two-thirds of
the loss from rising yields but only about half of the gain from
falling yields.

## Yield to call and yield to worst

Yield to maturity assumes the bond stays outstanding until maturity.
For a callable bond trading well above its call price that assumption
flatters the yield, because a call is likely to end the coupons early.

The **yield to call** (YTC) is the IRR if the bond is called on a
given call date. It solves

$$
P = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{c \cdot F}{(1+\text{YTC})^t} + \frac{K}{(1+\text{YTC})^n}.
$$

The **yield to worst** (YTW) is the lowest of the yield to maturity
and the yield to every possible call date. It is what the investor
earns if the issuer does whatever is worst for the investor.

**Worked example.** The baseline bond (6% coupon, 10 years, first
callable in year 3 at 102) trades at 104.

The yield to maturity solves

$$
104 = \sum_{t=1}^{10} \frac{6}{(1+\text{YTM})^t} + \frac{100}{(1+\text{YTM})^{10}},
$$

which gives a yield to maturity of 5.47%. The yield to the first call
date solves

$$
104 = \frac{6}{1+\text{YTC}} + \frac{6}{(1+\text{YTC})^2} + \frac{6 + 102}{(1+\text{YTC})^3},
$$

which gives a yield to call of 5.16%. As with any yield, you solve
these by trial and error or in a spreadsheet: `=RATE(10, 6, -104, 100)`
and `=RATE(3, 6, -104, 102)`. Later call dates give higher yields,
starting at 5.33% for a call in year 4.

So the yield to worst is 5.16%, 31 basis points below the yield to
maturity. An investor who buys at 104 and is repaid 102 in year 3
spreads a 2-point loss over three years. Held to maturity, a 4-point
loss is spread over ten.

Now suppose the same bond trades at 95. The yield to maturity is
6.70% and the yield to first call is 8.57%. A call would hand the
investor 102 for a bond bought at 95, so the worst case is no call at
all, and the yield to worst is the 6.70% yield to maturity.

For a single call price the pattern is this. At or below the call
price, the yield to worst is the yield to maturity. Well above the
call price, it is the yield to the first call date. Just above the
call price the yield to maturity can still be the lower of the two.
For this bond that holds up to a price of about 102.65: at 102.5 the
yield to maturity is 5.67% and the yield to first call is 5.70%. So
compute both and take the smaller.

## Who pays for the option

The option is valuable to the issuer, so investors do not give it
away. The chart shows the payment as a lower price: with the coupon
held at 6%, the callable bond sells for 102.55 where the straight bond
sells for 107.72. At issue the payment takes the other form. Both
sides know the bond is callable, and the investor is paid for writing
the call through a higher coupon.

An illustration from this lesson's model, not a market quote: with a
flat 5% yield and 15% rate volatility, a 10-year bond that cannot be
called sells at par with a 5% coupon. Make it callable from year 3 at
102 and it needs a coupon of about 5.43% to sell at par. The extra
0.43% a year is what the issuer pays for the right to refinance.

The terms of the call move that price. Longer call protection or a
higher call price makes the option worth less to the issuer, so the
extra coupon shrinks. With five years of call protection in place of
three, the par coupon in the same model is about 5.27%. More rate
volatility makes the option worth more, as the volatility slider
shows.

Why would an issuer pay for the option? If rates fall, or the firm's
credit improves, it can replace expensive debt with cheaper debt. A
call also lets the firm retire a bond whose covenants have started to
restrict it. If the option is fairly priced, it has zero NPV for both
sides at issue: the issuer pays for exactly what it gets.

## Make-whole calls

Many corporate bonds, investment-grade ones especially, carry a
different kind of call. A **make-whole call** has no fixed call price.
If the issuer calls, it pays the present value of all the remaining
coupons and the face value, discounted at the yield on a Treasury of
matching maturity plus a small spread written into the contract, and
never less than face value.

An illustrative example: a bond with a 5% coupon and 7 years left has
a make-whole spread of 0.25%. The matching Treasury yields 3%, and
investors price the bond at a 4% yield, a credit spread of 1%.

- Market value: the payments discounted at 4%, which is 106.00.
- Make-whole price: the same payments discounted at 3.25%, which is
  110.80.

The issuer would pay 110.80 to retire a bond worth 106.00. As long as
the make-whole spread is below the bond's credit spread, the
make-whole price is above the market value, and it rises when Treasury
yields fall. Falling rates therefore do not make this call a way to
refinance cheaply.

So a make-whole call puts no fixed ceiling on the price and creates
almost no negative convexity. It is worth little to the issuer as a
bet on interest rates, so it should add little to the coupon. Issuers
want it for flexibility. It lets a firm retire debt for a business
reason, such as a merger or a restructuring, by paying investors in
full.

## What to remember

- Callable bond = straight bond − a call option held by the issuer.
  The investor is short that call.
- When yields fall below the coupon rate, the price flattens near the
  price to call. That is negative convexity.
- Quote a callable bond on its yield to worst, the lowest of the yield
  to maturity and the yield to each call date. Well above the call
  price that is the yield to first call. At or below the call price it
  is the yield to maturity.
- The issuer pays for the option with a higher coupon.
- A make-whole call is priced off Treasury yields, so it does not cap
  the price.
