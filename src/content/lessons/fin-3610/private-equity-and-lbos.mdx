## The return on a buyout

A **leveraged buyout (LBO)** is the purchase of a whole company paid
for largely with borrowed money. The buyer is usually a **private
equity fund**: a pool of investors' money run by a specialist firm.
The fund puts in equity $E_0$, the company borrows the rest, and
the fund sells after $T$ years for equity worth $E_T$. Two numbers
summarize the result:

$$
\text{MOIC} = \frac{E_T}{E_0},
\qquad
\text{IRR} = \left(\frac{E_T}{E_0}\right)^{1/T} - 1.
$$

The **multiple of invested capital (MOIC)** says how many dollars
came back per dollar put in. The **internal rate of return (IRR)**
turns that into a yearly rate. This short formula works when there
is one payment in and one payment out. With payments along the way,
solve for the rate that sets NPV to zero, as in
[Investment Decision Rules](/lessons/fin-3610/investment-decision-rules).

MOIC ignores time and IRR does not. Doubling the money is 2.0x
whether it takes three years or seven, but the IRR is 26.0% over
three years, 14.9% over five, and 10.4% over seven.

Equity is what is left of the company's value after its debt:

$$
E = \text{EV} - D,
\qquad
\text{EV} = m \times \text{EBITDA}.
$$

EV is enterprise value, $D$ is debt net of cash, and $m$ is the
EV/EBITDA multiple from
[Multiples and Comparables](/lessons/fin-3610/multiples-and-comparables).
Buyers usually quote the price of a whole company as a multiple of
its EBITDA, so a buyout has an **entry multiple** $m_0$ and an
**exit multiple** $m_T$.

## Three sources of return

Write equity at exit and at entry with those two identities and
subtract. The gain splits into three parts:

$$
E_T - E_0 =
\underbrace{m_0\,(\text{EBITDA}_T - \text{EBITDA}_0)}_{\text{earnings growth}}
+ \underbrace{(m_T - m_0)\,\text{EBITDA}_T}_{\text{multiple change}}
+ \underbrace{(D_0 - D_T)}_{\text{debt paydown}}.
$$

- **Earnings growth.** The company earns more, and each extra dollar
  of EBITDA is worth $m_0$ dollars of value. This is the part the
  owners can work on directly.
- **Multiple change.** Buyers at exit pay a different price per
  dollar of EBITDA than the fund paid at entry. This depends mostly
  on market conditions at the two dates, and it is negative when
  multiples fall.
- **Debt paydown.** The company's own cash flow repays debt. Each
  dollar repaid is a dollar of company value that now belongs to the
  owners instead of the lenders. This part is not a gain from
  borrowing: with no debt the same cash would go straight to the
  owners, and more of it, because no interest is paid. Borrowing
  raises the percentage return by making $E_0$ smaller.

This split is called a **value bridge**.

## A worked example

These numbers are stylized, not a real deal. A fund buys a firm at
10 times its EBITDA of \$100M, so the price is \$1,000M. Ignore
fees. The company borrows \$500M at 8% and the fund puts in \$500M
of equity. EBITDA grows 5% a year. Half of each year's EBITDA is
cash left after taxes and investment. Interest on the year's opening
debt is paid first and the rest repays debt (\$ millions, rounded):

| Year | EBITDA | Cash flow | Interest | Debt repaid | Debt at year end |
| ---: | -----: | --------: | -------: | ----------: | ---------------: |
|    1 |  105.0 |      52.5 |     40.0 |        12.5 |            487.5 |
|    2 |  110.3 |      55.1 |     39.0 |        16.1 |            471.4 |
|    3 |  115.8 |      57.9 |     37.7 |        20.2 |            451.2 |
|    4 |  121.6 |      60.8 |     36.1 |        24.7 |            426.5 |
|    5 |  127.6 |      63.8 |     34.1 |        29.7 |            396.8 |

The fund sells at the end of year 5 for 10 times \$127.6M, which is
\$1,276M. After repaying the \$397M of debt still owed, equity is
\$879M.

$$
\text{MOIC} = \frac{879}{500} = 1.76,
\qquad
\text{IRR} = 1.76^{1/5} - 1 = 12.0\%.
$$

The value bridge: earnings growth is $10 \times (127.6 - 100) = 276$,
the multiple did not change, and debt fell by $500 - 397 = 103$.
The total, 379, is the gain in equity from 500 to 879. About
three quarters of it came from growth.

**unlevered IRR**, the return on the same purchase made with no
debt, which the next section explains.

## Why leverage raises both the expected return and the risk

Run the same purchase with no debt. The fund puts in the whole
\$1,000M, takes each year's cash flow as it is earned (\$290M in
total) and sells for \$1,276M. That is 1.57x. With payments along
the way the short IRR formula does not apply. Each year the company
pays out 5.25% of its opening value and its value grows 5%, so the
fund earns 10.25% a year. Now compare the two versions across
outcomes:

| Outcome                                | No debt | Debt of 5x EBITDA at 8% |
| -------------------------------------- | ------: | ----------------------: |
| EBITDA grows 10% a year, exit at 12x   |   19.2% |                   26.0% |
| Baseline: grows 5% a year, exit at 10x |   10.3% |                   12.0% |
| No growth, exit at 10x                 |    5.0% |                    2.2% |
| EBITDA falls 5% a year, exit at 8x     |   −4.0% |                  −22.6% |

The lenders are owed a fixed 8% whatever happens. When the company
earns more than 8% on its value, the extra goes to the owners, and
it is spread over a smaller equity stake. When the company earns
less, the gap comes out of the owners too. So debt widens the range
of equity returns in both directions. Averaged over outcomes, the
levered return is higher only if the company is expected to earn
more than its debt costs. This is Proposition II from
[Capital Structure in a Perfect Market](/lessons/fin-3610/mm-perfect-market):

$$
r_E = r_U + \frac{D}{E}\,(r_U - r_D).
$$

Year 1 of the baseline: $10.25\% + \frac{500}{500}\,(10.25\% - 8\%) = 12.5\%$.
The worked example agrees: equity goes from 500 to
$10 \times 105 - 487.5 = 562.5$. As debt is repaid, $D/E$ falls and
each later year earns a little less. The five years average 12.0%.

The same lesson carries a warning. In a perfect market the higher
expected return on levered equity is exactly the pay for its higher
risk, so leverage alone creates no value. Real markets depart from
that in ways that matter for a buyout:

- **Taxes add value.** Interest is deductible, so debt shields
  income from tax. See
  [Debt and Taxes](/lessons/fin-3610/debt-and-taxes). The chart
  leaves this out: its cash flow is the same share of EBITDA at any
  level of debt.
- **Distress destroys value.** A company with heavy debt has little
  room for a bad year. If cash flow cannot cover interest it
  defaults, and default is costly. See
  [Financial Distress and Trade-Off Theory](/lessons/fin-3610/financial-distress).
  In the chart, push debt to 7x at a 10% rate: the warning appears
  in year 1 and the IRR drops below the baseline.
- **Control can add value.** A single owner with control can replace
  managers and change strategy. Any gain shows up in the earnings
  growth bar, not the debt bar.

## Sources and uses of funds

Every buyout starts from a table that must balance. **Uses** are
what the money pays for. **Sources** are where it comes from. Here
is a real one. Investors led by KKR bought Dollar General in July
2007, paying shareholders \$22.00 a share.

| Sources of funds              | \$ millions | Uses of funds           | \$ millions |
| ----------------------------- | ----------: | ----------------------- | ----------: |
| Revolving credit facility     |       432.3 | Purchase price          |     7,024.9 |
| Term loan                     |     2,300.0 | Rollover equity         |         3.2 |
| Senior notes, net of discount |     1,151.8 | Refinance existing debt |       215.6 |
| Senior subordinated notes     |       725.0 | Other retained debt     |        66.7 |
| Other retained debt           |        66.7 | Fees and expenses       |       287.0 |
| Equity contribution           |     2,767.0 |                         |             |
| Rollover equity (management)  |         3.2 |                         |             |
| Excess cash on hand           |       151.4 |                         |             |
| **Total sources**             | **7,597.4** | **Total uses**          | **7,597.4** |

_Source: Dollar General Corporation, Amendment No. 1 to Form S-4,
filed with the SEC on January 25, 2008, section "The Transactions"
([SEC EDGAR](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/29534/000104746908000524/a2181974zs-4a.htm)).
Row labels are shortened. The facts below are from the same filing._

How to read it:

- **Debt is 61.5% of the money.** The five debt lines add to
  \$4,675.8 million. Equity is \$2,770.2 million, or 36.5%, and the
  company's own cash covers the last 2.0%. That is \$1.69 of debt
  for each dollar of equity.
- **The company owes the debt, not the fund.** Dollar General's
  stores repay the lenders out of their cash flow. If the company
  fails, the fund loses its equity and owes nothing more.
- **Debt comes in layers.** The revolving facility (a credit line
  the company draws on and repays as needed) and the term loan
  (borrowed in full on day one, due in seven years) are secured and
  are repaid first. The senior notes are bonds paying 10.625%. Their
  face value is \$1,175 million and they brought in \$1,151.8
  million, which is what "net of discount" means. The senior
  subordinated notes are last in line among the lenders. They pay
  11.875% in cash, or 12.625% if the company chooses to pay with
  more notes instead. A place further back in line recovers less in
  a default, so it earns a higher rate. That is the recovery term in
  the spread formula of
  [Credit Risk and Spreads](/lessons/fin-3610/credit-risk-and-spreads).
- **Two lines sit on both sides.** Senior managers invested \$3.2
  million, by keeping shares they already held (a rollover) or by
  paying cash, and \$66.7 million of old debt stayed in place. Each
  appears as a source and as a use.
- **Fees are a use of funds.** The \$287.0 million of fees and
  expenses, 3.8% of the total, paid banks, lawyers and advisers,
  including firms tied to the buyers.

## Who gets the return: fund structure and fees

A buyout fund is a limited partnership with two kinds of partner.

- **Limited partners (LPs)** supply most of the money: pension
  funds, university endowments, insurers and wealthy individuals.
  They make no investment decisions and can lose no more than they
  commit. Funds typically invest over a horizon of 10 or more years
  and limit withdrawals along the way, according to the
  [SEC's investor guide](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/private-investment-funds/private-equity).
- **The general partner (GP)** is the private equity firm. It picks
  the companies, arranges the debt, sits on the boards and decides
  when to sell.

The GP is paid in two ways. With fee rate $f$ and carry rate $c$:

$$
\text{fee per year} = f \times \text{committed capital},
\qquad
\text{carry} = c \times (\text{proceeds} - \text{capital paid in}).
$$

Committed capital is the total the LPs have promised. Capital paid
in is what they have handed over so far, fees included.

The **management fee** is paid whatever the results. **Carried
interest** is a share of the profit with a condition attached: after
the carry is paid, the LPs must still hold their capital plus a
**hurdle** return of $h$ a year, which is
$\text{capital paid in} \times (1 + h)^T$ after $T$ years. If
proceeds are below that amount there is no carry. Above it, the GP
takes the smaller of two amounts: its share $c$ of the profit, or
whatever the proceeds exceed the hurdle amount by. This rule is
called a full catch-up.

Terms differ from fund to fund. A common shorthand is "2 and 20",
meaning $f = 2\%$ and $c = 20\%$. The example below uses those
rates and assumes a hurdle of $h = 8\%$, with made-up fund numbers.

LPs commit \$1,000M to a fund that lasts five years (shorter than a
real fund, to keep the arithmetic small).

1. **Fees.** $2\% \times 1{,}000 = 20$ a year, or \$100M over five
   years. That leaves \$900M to invest in companies.
2. **Proceeds.** The companies are sold at the end of year 5 for
   \$2,000M.
3. **Carry.** The LPs paid in \$1,000M, fees included, so the profit
   is 1,000 and $20\% \times 1{,}000 = 200$. The GP would take
   \$200M and the LPs \$1,800M.
4. **Hurdle.** The LPs need $1{,}000 \times 1.08^5 = 1{,}469$. They
   hold 1,800 after the carry, so the full \$200M is paid.

The LPs earn 1.8x, an IRR of 12.5% if we treat the \$1,000M as paid
in on day one. The companies themselves turned \$900M into \$2,000M,
which is 2.22x and 17.3% a year. Fees and carry cost the LPs almost
5 percentage points a year, and the GP collects \$300M. The chart's
IRR is the company-level kind, before fund fees and carry.

Two other outcomes show the hurdle at work. Had the companies sold
for \$1,400M, below the \$1,469M hurdle, there would be no carry.
At \$1,500M, 20% of the \$500M profit is \$100M, but paying it would
leave the LPs \$1,400M, below the hurdle, so the GP takes only
$1{,}500 - 1{,}469 = 31$. In both cases the GP keeps the \$100M of
fees.

The company that was bought can pay fees as well. Dollar General
agreed to pay firms tied to its buyers \$5 million a year, rising 5%
annually, under a monitoring agreement.

## Buyouts and mergers

A buyout is an acquisition, so the logic of
[Mergers and Acquisitions](/lessons/fin-3610/mergers-and-acquisitions)
applies: the buyer pays a premium over the market price, and the
deal creates value for the buyer only if the company is worth more
in its hands than the price paid. The difference is the kind of
buyer. A company buying a rival can hope for synergies with its own
business. A fund usually has no business to combine with the target,
so it must earn its return from the three sources above: growing
EBITDA, selling at a better multiple than it paid, and using the
company's cash flow to repay debt.

That is why the entry multiple matters so much. In the chart, raise
the entry multiple from 10x to 12x and leave the rest alone: the
IRR falls from 12.0% to 4.7%. The company and the sale are the same.
Only the price changed.
