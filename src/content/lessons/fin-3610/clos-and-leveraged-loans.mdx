## A company that only owns loans

A company bought in a leveraged buyout borrows a large part of its
purchase price, the subject of the lesson on private equity and LBOs.
This lesson follows that loan to its most common owner.

A **collateralized loan obligation (CLO)** is a company with one
activity. It buys loans to roughly 200 companies and pays for them by
selling its own notes, which are bonds. An asset management firm, the
CLO manager, picks the loans and is paid a fee out of the pool. The
notes come in layers called **tranches**, ranked from AAA at the top
down to an unrated equity tranche at the bottom. Turning a pool of
loans into ranked securities this way is called **securitization**.

Cash from the loans is paid out from the top down. If the loans do not
pay enough, the tranche paid last falls short first, so losses climb
from the bottom up.

Two equations describe the losses. If a fraction $d$ of the pool
defaults over the life of the deal, and lenders recover a fraction $R$
of each defaulted loan, the pool loses a fraction

$$
L = d \times (1 - R)
$$

of its principal. Let tranche $i$ have size $S_i$, and let the tranches
ranked below it add up to $A_i$, both as fractions of the pool. Its
loss is

$$
\text{loss}_i = \min\bigl(S_i,\ \max(0,\ L - A_i)\bigr).
$$

The tranche loses nothing while $L$ is below $A_i$, loses dollar for
dollar after that, and is wiped out once $L$ reaches $A_i + S_i$. The
cushion $A_i$ is the tranche's **subordination**: what has to burn
before the tranche is touched. This loss is a fraction of the pool.
Divide it by $S_i$ for the share of the tranche's own principal that
is lost.

The first equation is the expected-loss logic from
[Credit Risk and Spreads](/lessons/fin-3610/credit-risk-and-spreads),
applied to a pool. There $p$ was one bond's chance of default in a
year. Here $d$ is the share of the whole pool that defaults over the
deal's life, and $R$ is the recovery rate written $\delta$ there.

This lesson uses one illustrative funding mix throughout:

| Tranche | Share of funding $S_i$ | Subordination $A_i$ |
| ------- | ---------------------- | ------------------- |
| AAA     | 62%                    | 38%                 |
| AA      | 12%                    | 26%                 |
| A       | 6%                     | 20%                 |
| BBB     | 6%                     | 14%                 |
| BB      | 5%                     | 9%                  |
| Equity  | 9%                     | 0%                  |

Suppose 20% of the loans default and lenders recover 65% of each.
Then $L = 0.20 \times 0.35 = 7\%$. The equity tranche loses
$7 / 9 = 78\%$ of its principal, and every note is repaid in full.

The mix is illustrative but close to reported averages. Federal

(DeMarco, Liu, and Schmidt-Eisenlohr 2020). In U.S. CLOs issued from
2010 to 2019, AAA notes averaged 61% of the funding and all notes
together 89% (Cordell, Roberts, and Schwert 2021).

## What the pool holds: leveraged loans

A **leveraged loan** is a loan to a company that already carries a lot
of debt. Three features define it:

- **Floating rate.** The borrower pays a benchmark rate plus a fixed
  spread, reset every few months. In the U.S. the benchmark is SOFR
  (the Secured Overnight Financing Rate), which replaced LIBOR
  in 2023. A loan at SOFR + 3.5% pays 7.5% when SOFR is 4%.
- **Senior secured.** The lenders hold a first claim on the borrower's
  assets, so in a bankruptcy they are paid before unsecured
  bondholders.
- **Below investment grade.** The borrower is rated BB+ or lower, the
  speculative side of the line drawn in Credit Risk and Spreads, or is
  not rated at all.

Companies bought by private equity firms are typical borrowers. Banks
arrange the loans and sell them on, and CLOs are the main buyers. A
2019 Federal Reserve staff note reports that U.S. CLOs account for
about half of U.S. leveraged loans outstanding, with \$617 billion of
CLO securities outstanding at the end of 2018 (Liu and
Schmidt-Eisenlohr 2019).

Cordell, Roberts, and Schwert (2021) describe the typical pool. It
holds floating-rate, senior secured loans with original maturities of
five to seven years, made to 150 to 250 different borrowers. The deal
contract caps any one company at 2% of the pool and any one industry
at 15%. In deals issued since 2010, the median loan is rated B.

The CLO's own notes also pay a floating rate. A rise in SOFR raises
what the CLO collects and what it owes by about the same amount, so
the deal carries credit risk and very little interest rate risk. That
risk has not vanished. It sits with the borrowers, whose interest bill
rises with SOFR.

## The order of payments

Every quarter the CLO collects interest from its loans and pays it out
in a fixed order called the **waterfall**:

1. Administrative expenses and the manager's senior fee.
2. Interest on the AAA notes, then AA, A, BBB, and BB, in that order.
3. The manager's subordinated fee.
4. Whatever is left, to the equity tranche.

Principal follows the same ranking with a difference in timing. For
the first four to five years, called the reinvestment period, the
manager uses principal repaid by borrowers to buy new loans. After
that, repaid principal retires the notes from the top down: AAA first,
equity last (Cordell, Roberts, and Schwert 2021).

Each tranche is paid only after everything above it is paid in full.
Senior notes therefore have the safest claim and the lowest interest
rate. Equity has no promised payment at all.

Each layer has its own buyers. Of the CLO securities held by U.S.
banks at the end of 2018, 95% were senior notes (DeMarco, Liu, and
Schmidt-Eisenlohr 2020). Capital rules make low-rated assets costly
for banks and insurers to hold, so a AAA note backed by risky loans
suits them where the loans themselves would not (Cordell, Roberts, and
Schwert 2021). Hedge funds and the CLO managers themselves are typical
holders of the riskiest notes and the equity (Liu and
Schmidt-Eisenlohr 2019).

## Coverage tests

The waterfall has tripwires called **coverage tests**. The main one is
the overcollateralization (OC) test, run for each class of notes. It
divides the principal of the loan pool by the principal of that class
and every class above it:

$$
\text{OC}_i = \frac{\text{loan pool}}{\text{notes at or above } i}.
$$

The deal contract sets a minimum for each class. Take the illustrative
mix on a \$500 million pool, so the notes from AAA through BB total
\$455 million, and suppose the BB minimum is 105% (illustrative). At
the start $\text{OC}_{BB} = 500 / 455 = 109.9\%$, and the test passes.

Defaulted loans are counted below face value. Suppose \$60 million of
loans default and the contract counts them at half of face value until
the actual recovery is known. The pool now counts as \$470 million,
and $\text{OC}_{BB} = 470 / 455 = 103.3\%$. The test fails.

A failed test changes the waterfall. Interest that would have flowed
to the claims below the failed test is used to repay principal on the
most senior notes until the test passes again. Here the notes must
shrink to $470 / 1.05 = 447.6$ million. So \$7.4 million of interest
that would have gone to the subordinated fee and the equity is paid to
the AAA holders instead, over as many quarters as it takes. An
interest coverage test works the same way, comparing interest
collected with interest owed.

Coverage tests make the deal pay down its own debt when the pool gets
worse, before losses reach the notes. The cost falls on the equity. In
the 2008 financial crisis, payments to equity fell to zero in the
majority of CLOs for this reason (Cordell, Roberts, and Schwert 2021).

## Why AAA notes can sit on B-rated loans

No loan in the pool is rated anywhere near AAA. The rating on the
senior notes comes from the structure, through three protections:

- **Diversification.** With 200 equal loans, one default costs the
  pool $0.5\% \times (1 - R)$. No single borrower can sink the deal.
- **Subordination.** In the illustrative mix, 38% of the funding
  absorbs losses before the AAA notes lose a dollar.
- **Excess interest.** The loans pay more interest than the notes
  cost, and the coverage tests turn that surplus into repayment of
  senior notes when the pool weakens.

The record so far supports the rating. Of 1,250 AAA-rated CLO tranches
issued from 1997 to 2009, none had defaulted by March 2021, a period
that includes the 2008 crisis. Of the tranches rated BB or B from
those years, 3.33% defaulted (Cordell, Roberts, and Schwert 2021,
compiled from rating-agency reports).

### What breaks it: defaults that arrive together

Diversification works only if loans default for separate reasons. Give
each of 200 loans a 20% chance of defaulting over the deal's life
(illustrative). If the defaults are independent, the pool's default
rate $d$ has a mean of 20% and a standard deviation of

$$
\sqrt{\frac{0.20 \times 0.80}{200}} = 2.8\%.
$$

Two standard deviations either way puts $d$ between 14.3% and 25.7%.
At 65% recovery the pool loss stays between 5.0% and 9.0%: the bad end
uses up the equity tranche and stops there. Wiping out the BB tranche
as well ($L = 14\%$) would take $d = 40\%$, seven standard deviations
above the mean.

Now suppose one shock, such as a deep recession, decides the fate of
all 200 borrowers at once. The average default rate is still 20%, but
it arrives as an 80% chance of no defaults and a 20% chance that every
loan defaults. In the bad case $L = 35\%$ at 65% recovery: the equity,
BB, BBB, and A tranches are wiped out, and the AA tranche loses 75% of
its principal. Recoveries also tend to fall when many firms fail
together. At 40% recovery $L = 60\%$, and the AAA notes lose
$(60 - 38) / 62 = 35\%$ of their principal.

Real pools sit between these two cases. The borrowers are in different
industries, but they are all heavily indebted companies paying
floating rates in the same economy. The AAA rating therefore rests on
an estimate of how correlated the loans are, and that correlation
cannot be observed directly. Loan quality matters too. Loans without
maintenance covenants, the financial tests a borrower must keep
passing, rose from 20% of leveraged loans in 2012 to 80% in 2018.
Weaker terms delay defaults but raise the loss when one comes (BIS
2019).

## What the equity tranche earns

The equity tranche has no promised interest. It receives what is left:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{equity income} = {} & \text{loan interest} - \text{fees} \\
& - \text{note interest}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Take a \$500 million pool funded with the illustrative mix, so the
equity is \$45 million. The loans pay SOFR + 3.5%, and SOFR is 4%.
Fees are 0.5% of the pool a year, a typical total for the manager's
senior and subordinated fees (Cordell, Roberts, and Schwert 2021). The
note spreads below are illustrative.

| Notes     | Size (\$ millions) | Rate                | Interest (\$ millions) |
| --------- | ------------------ | ------------------- | ---------------------- |
| AAA       | 310                | SOFR + 1.3% = 5.3%  | 16.430                 |
| AA        | 60                 | SOFR + 1.8% = 5.8%  | 3.480                  |
| A         | 30                 | SOFR + 2.3% = 6.3%  | 1.890                  |
| BBB       | 30                 | SOFR + 3.3% = 7.3%  | 2.190                  |
| BB        | 25                 | SOFR + 6.5% = 10.5% | 2.625                  |
| All notes | 455                |                     | 26.615                 |

In millions of dollars:

- Loan interest: $7.5\% \times 500 = 37.5$.
- Fees: $0.5\% \times 500 = 2.5$.
- Note interest: $26.615$.
- Equity income: $37.5 - 2.5 - 26.615 = 8.385$, a cash yield of
  $8.385 / 45 = 18.6\%$.

That 18.6% is before any loan loses money. Equity is 9% of the funding
and takes the first loss, so each 1% of the pool that is lost costs
the equity $1 / 0.09 = 11$ percentage points. If 2% of the loans
default in a year and 65% is recovered, the pool loses 0.7%, which is
\$3.5 million. The equity's return after losses that year is
$(8.385 - 3.5) / 45 = 10.9\%$.

Kept up for ten years, that pace is the chart's baseline: 20%
cumulative defaults, a 7% pool loss, and 78% of the equity's principal
gone. The equity's bar in the chart looks grim, but its holders were
paid interest every year along the way.

The return comes from a gap you met in Credit Risk and Spreads. Risky
loans pay a spread larger than their expected loss, while most of the
CLO's funding is AAA debt that pays a small spread. Equity holders
collect the gap on about 11 dollars of loans for every dollar they put
in, and they bear the first loss on all of it. That is leveraged
credit risk.

Among CLOs issued from 1997 to 2016 and fully repaid by March 2021,
the equity tranche earned an average internal rate of return of 9.9%
after fees: 13.0% in deals issued before 2010 and 4.9% in later ones.
The authors caution that the later figure is likely too low, because
the recent deals that had already finished were mostly ones liquidated
early (Cordell, Roberts, and Schwert 2021).

## How this differs from the mortgage CDOs of 2008

The collateralized debt obligations (CDOs) at the center of the 2008
crisis used the same design: a pool, tranches, and a waterfall. Their
AAA tranches defaulted widely. CLO AAA tranches did not. A 2019 study
by the Bank for International Settlements (BIS) points to four
differences:

- **What is in the pool.** Almost 70% of the assets backing CDOs
  issued in 2006 were subprime mortgage securities, which are bonds
  backed by home loans to weak borrowers, and another 15% were other
  CDOs. Nearly everything depended on U.S. house prices. A CLO holds
  loans to companies spread across industries.
- **Layers.** Cut another way, more than 40% of those 2006 assets were
  not securities at all but credit default swaps, which are side bets
  on mortgage securities. A CLO holds the actual loans, with little
  repackaging.
- **Funding.** Banks held many CDO tranches in off-balance-sheet
  vehicles financed with short-term borrowing that lenders could pull.
  Holders of CLO notes rarely borrow short-term against them, and the
  CLO's own notes are long-term: investors cannot redeem them early
  (Liu and Schmidt-Eisenlohr 2019).
- **Cushion.** Junior and middle tranches make up a larger share of a
  CLO than they did of a pre-crisis subprime CDO. CLOs added more
  after the crisis: the AAA share of funding fell from 72% in deals
  issued from 1997 to 2009 to 61% in deals issued from 2010 to 2019
  (Cordell, Roberts, and Schwert 2021).

The comparison is not a guarantee. The same BIS study warns that the
safety of senior CLO notes rests on how correlated loan losses turn
out to be, the weak point described above. A clean record through two
downturns is evidence, not proof.
