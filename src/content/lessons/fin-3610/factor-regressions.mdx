## The regression

The last lesson stated the factor models. This one estimates them.
For a portfolio $p$ observed every month $t$, the three-factor
regression is

$$
R_{p,t} - R_{f,t} = \alpha_p + \beta_M (R_{m,t} - R_{f,t}) + \beta_S \, \text{SMB}_t + \beta_V \, \text{HML}_t + \varepsilon_{p,t}.
$$

Everything with a $t$ subscript except $\varepsilon_{p,t}$ is data:
the portfolio's return, the T-bill rate $R_f$, and the factor returns
for that month. The unknowns are $\alpha_p$ and the betas. Ordinary least squares (OLS)
picks the values that make the sum of squared residuals
$\varepsilon_{p,t}$ as small as possible.

The same template gives all three models in this lesson:

- **1-factor (CAPM)**: keep only the market term.
- **3-factor**: market, SMB and HML, as written above.
- **5-factor**: add RMW (profitability) and CMA (investment).

## What the coefficients measure

**Beta** is a slope. A market beta of 1.2 says: in a month when the
market's excess return is 1 percentage point higher, the portfolio's
excess return is 1.2 points higher on average, holding the other
factors fixed.

**Alpha** is what is left over. The OLS fit always passes through
the sample averages, so

$$
\alpha_p = \overline{R_p - R_f} - \beta_M \, \overline{R_m - R_f} - \beta_S \, \overline{\text{SMB}} - \beta_V \, \overline{\text{HML}},
$$

where a bar means the average over the sample. Alpha is the average
excess return that the factor exposures do not account for. If the
model prices the portfolio correctly, alpha is zero. A positive
alpha means the portfolio earned more than its exposures justify
under that model.

## Reading the output

Regression software prints one row per coefficient with three
numbers in it, plus one number for the regression as a whole.

- **Coefficient**: the estimate of alpha or of a beta.
- **Standard error**: the typical size of the estimation error. A
  rough 95% confidence interval is the coefficient plus or minus two
  standard errors.
- **t-statistic**: the coefficient divided by its standard error,
  so it counts how many standard errors the estimate sits from zero.
  When it is above about 2 in absolute value (1.96 in a large
  sample), you reject "the true value is zero" at the 5% level.
- **R-squared**: the share of the variance of the portfolio's excess
  return that the factors explain. It measures how closely the
  portfolio moves with the factors. It does not tell you whether
  alpha is zero.

The t-statistic in the table tests against zero. For a market beta
the natural benchmark is usually one, so compute
$(\hat\beta_M - 1) / \text{SE}$ yourself.

## Example 1: CAPM

Small value stocks, one factor. Alpha is in percent per month.

| Term   | Coefficient | Std. error | t-statistic |
| ------ | ----------: | ---------: | ----------: |
| Alpha  |       0.326 |      0.117 |        2.78 |
| Market |       1.072 |      0.026 |       41.09 |

$R^2 = 0.690$. The t-statistics are computed from unrounded
estimates, so dividing the rounded columns gives slightly different
numbers.

The market beta is 1.07. Tested against one, $(1.072 - 1) / 0.026
\approx 2.8$, so it is slightly but reliably above one. Alpha is
0.326% per month, about 3.9% per year, with a t-statistic of 2.78.
CAPM says this alpha should be zero, and the data reject that. This
is the value anomaly from the last lesson, stated as one number.

You can rebuild alpha from averages. The portfolio's average excess
return is 0.969% per month and the market's is 0.600%, so

$$
\alpha = 0.969 - 1.072 \times 0.600 = 0.969 - 0.643 = 0.326.
$$

The R-squared of 0.69 says the market explains 69% of the
month-to-month variance. The rest comes from something the market
factor does not capture.

## Example 2: three factors

The same portfolio, with SMB and HML added.

| Term        | Coefficient | Std. error | t-statistic |
| ----------- | ----------: | ---------: | ----------: |
| Alpha       |       0.036 |      0.021 |        1.72 |
| Market      |       0.998 |      0.005 |      202.64 |
| Size (SMB)  |       0.885 |      0.007 |      122.95 |
| Value (HML) |       0.713 |      0.007 |       99.78 |

$R^2 = 0.990$.

The portfolio loads heavily on both new factors, as a small value
portfolio should. The market beta is now 0.998, which cannot be told
from one: $(0.998 - 1) / 0.005 = -0.4$. Alpha falls from 0.326 to
0.036, and with a t-statistic of 1.72 you can no longer reject zero. The decomposition
shows where the old alpha went. With average monthly factor returns
of 0.600 (market), 0.143 (SMB) and 0.292 (HML):

$$
0.969 \approx \underbrace{0.036}_{\alpha} + \underbrace{0.998 \times 0.600}_{0.599} + \underbrace{0.885 \times 0.143}_{0.127} + \underbrace{0.713 \times 0.292}_{0.208}.
$$

The size and value terms add $0.127 + 0.208 = 0.335$ per month. The
market term falls by 0.044, from 0.643 to 0.599, because the market
beta drops from 1.072 to 0.998. Alpha falls by the difference, about
0.29. The three-factor model reads CAPM's alpha as payment for size
and value exposure.

One caution about the R-squared of 0.99. Small value is one of the
six portfolios that SMB and HML are built from, so a near-perfect
fit is partly by construction. Industry portfolios are a fairer
test: for utilities the same step raises R-squared only from 0.33
to 0.41.

## Example 3: five factors

A different portfolio, small growth stocks, under the five-factor
model.

| Term                | Coefficient | Std. error | t-statistic |
| ------------------- | ----------: | ---------: | ----------: |
| Alpha               |      −0.079 |      0.032 |       −2.45 |
| Market              |       1.056 |      0.008 |      135.66 |
| Size (SMB)          |       1.016 |      0.011 |       90.51 |
| Value (HML)         |      −0.338 |      0.015 |      −23.27 |
| Profitability (RMW) |      −0.191 |      0.015 |      −13.09 |
| Investment (CMA)    |      −0.093 |      0.022 |       −4.32 |

$R^2 = 0.984$.

Read the signs. A negative HML beta marks a growth portfolio. A
negative RMW beta says it moves like firms with weak profits, and a
negative CMA beta says it moves like firms that invest aggressively.
All three factors have positive average returns, so each negative
beta lowers the return the model predicts. That is how the
five-factor model accounts for part of small growth's poor record:
alpha is $-0.169$ under three factors and $-0.079$ under five.

It does not account for all of it. The t-statistic is still $-2.45$.
Fama and French's own 2015 paper ("A five-factor asset pricing
model," _Journal of Financial Economics_) reports the same problem:
the five-factor model cannot explain the low average returns of
small stocks that move like low-profit, high-investment firms.

One data detail. The five-factor rows use French's five-factor file,
whose SMB averages over sorts on book-to-market, profitability and
investment. The three-factor file's SMB uses the book-to-market sort
only. That is mainly why small value's R-squared can slip from 0.990 with
three factors to 0.989 with five, which could not happen if the
second regression only added columns to the first.

## Does alpha shrink as factors are added?

Alpha in percent per month, with its t-statistic in parentheses.

| Portfolio    |          CAPM |      3-factor |      5-factor |
| ------------ | ------------: | ------------: | ------------: |
| Small value  |   0.33 (2.78) |   0.04 (1.72) |   0.03 (1.22) |
| Small growth | −0.23 (−1.84) | −0.17 (−4.20) | −0.08 (−2.45) |
| Big value    |   0.20 (2.19) | −0.09 (−2.23) | −0.03 (−0.76) |
| Big growth   |   0.00 (0.08) |   0.11 (4.47) |   0.08 (3.28) |
| Utilities    |   0.15 (1.24) |   0.04 (0.31) | −0.07 (−0.64) |
| High tech    |   0.03 (0.27) |   0.23 (2.28) |   0.40 (4.12) |

**For the value portfolios, yes.** Small value and big value both
have CAPM alphas with t-statistics above 2. Three factors overshoot
for big value ($t = -2.23$). With five factors both are within one
and a quarter standard errors of zero.

**Not for every portfolio.** High tech has almost no alpha under
CAPM and an alpha of 0.40% per month, about 4.8% per year, under
five factors. Its betas on HML, RMW and CMA are all negative, so the
five-factor model predicts a low return. The industry earned a high
one, and the gap shows up as alpha. Big growth shows a milder
version: no alpha under CAPM, and 0.11% per month ($t = 4.47$) under
three factors. Adding factors changes the benchmark; it does not
guarantee a smaller alpha.

**A smaller alpha can be a more significant one.** Small growth's
alpha shrinks in size from $-0.23$ to $-0.17$ between CAPM and three
factors, yet its t-statistic goes from $-1.84$ to $-4.20$. The added
factors explain much more of the portfolio's variance, so the
standard error of alpha falls from 0.122 to 0.040.

## From betas to a cost of equity

A factor model turns betas into an expected return the same way
CAPM does, with one term per factor:

$$
r_E = R_f + \beta_M \lambda_M + \beta_S \lambda_S + \beta_V \lambda_V,
$$

where each $\lambda$ is the expected annual premium on that factor.
There is no alpha term: a forecast assumes the model holds, and a
past alpha is not a promise.

Take illustrative inputs: $R_f = 4\%$ and a market premium of
$5.5\%$, the middle of the 5 to 6% working range from the cost of
capital lesson. The earlier lessons give no working range for SMB
and HML, so use their averages in this sample, 1.7% and 3.5% per year (12
times the monthly averages). Betas come from the full-sample
regressions.

| Industry  | CAPM                           | 3-factor                                                          |
| --------- | ------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Utilities | $4 + 0.52 \times 5.5 = 6.9\%$  | $4 + 0.61 \times 5.5 - 0.20 \times 1.7 + 0.32 \times 3.5 = 8.1\%$ |
| High tech | $4 + 1.23 \times 5.5 = 10.8\%$ | $4 + 1.12 \times 5.5 + 0.17 \times 1.7 - 0.54 \times 3.5 = 8.6\%$ |

Utilities move like value stocks (a positive HML beta), so the
three-factor model adds a bit more than one point to their cost of
equity. High tech moves like growth stocks (a negative HML beta), so
it loses about 2.2 points. The two industries are 3.9 points apart
under CAPM and about half a point apart under three factors.

Treat the factor version as a cross-check, not a replacement. The
SMB and HML premia are historical averages, and the same sample's
market average is 7.2% per year (12 times the monthly average over
one-month T-bills), well above the 5 to 6% working range. Historical
averages can overstate what investors expect going forward. The
split-sample table below shows the value effect fading after 1995,
so 3.5% for HML is probably generous. CAPM stays the default; the factor estimate tells you which
direction CAPM may be missing.

## Limits

**Estimation error.** With 759 months, betas are tight. The
utilities CAPM beta is 0.523 with a standard error of 0.027, so a
rough 95% interval is 0.47 to 0.58. Practice usually has far less
data. Rerun the same regression on the last 60 months (October 2021
to September 2026) and the beta is 0.55 with a standard error of
0.11: an interval of 0.33 to 0.77. At a 4% risk-free rate and a
5.5% market premium that is a cost of equity anywhere from 5.8% to
8.2%. Alpha is harder still.
Its 60-month standard error is 0.51% per month, more than 6% per
year, so five years of monthly data cannot tell an alpha of several
percent a year from zero.

**Sample choice.** The answer depends on the window. Split the
sample in half and rerun CAPM:

| CAPM estimate         | Jul 1963 to Jan 1995 | Feb 1995 to Sep 2026 |
| --------------------- | -------------------: | -------------------: |
| Small value alpha (t) |          0.54 (3.69) |          0.11 (0.59) |
| Utilities market beta |                 0.63 |                 0.42 |
| High tech market beta |                 1.12 |                 1.34 |

The value anomaly is strong in the first half and cannot be told
from zero in the second. Betas drift too. A long sample averages
over conditions that may no longer hold; a short one is noisy. No
window fixes both problems.

**The standard errors rest on assumptions.** The ones reported here
assume the residuals have constant variance and are unrelated from
month to month. Stock volatility clusters in time, so published
studies usually report robust standard errors, which allow for it.
