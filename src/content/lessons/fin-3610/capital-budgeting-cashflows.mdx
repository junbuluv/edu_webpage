## The cash flow we need

NPV uses **free cash flow (FCF)** — the cash a project actually
generates that can be paid to debt or equity holders. Starting from
operating projections:

$$
\text{FCF} = \text{NOPAT} + \text{D\&A} - \Delta\text{WC} - \text{CapEx}.
$$

Where:

- **NOPAT** = EBIT × (1 − tax rate) when EBIT is positive. Operating
  profit, after taxes, before any financing costs. The simulation sets
  current tax to zero when EBIT is negative; multiplying a loss by $(1-t)$
  would instead assume the project can use the tax shield immediately
  against other taxable income. (Net income would also subtract
  interest expense; FCF deliberately excludes interest because the
  cost of debt is captured in the discount rate, not the cash flows.)
- **D&A** added back because it's a non-cash deduction.
- **ΔWC** subtracted because growing receivables and inventory tie up
  cash even though they don't show up in net income that year.
- **CapEx** subtracted in the year the cash leaves the firm.

In practice this is a two-step forecast. First project the project's
**incremental earnings** (revenue, costs, depreciation, taxes). Then
convert those earnings to free cash flow with the adjustments above.
Earnings are an accounting story; free cash flow is the cash the
project actually throws off, and only the cash drives NPV.

Keep gross profit and operating profit distinct:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{EBITDA}
  &= \text{Revenue} - \text{COGS} - \text{Cash operating expenses}, \\
\text{EBIT}
  &= \text{EBITDA} - \text{D\&A}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Gross margin determines revenue minus COGS. It is not an EBITDA
margin unless cash operating expenses are zero.

## What to include — and what not to

The principles for what counts as an _incremental_ cash flow:

**1. Include opportunity costs.** If the project uses a building you
already own, the cash flow to include is what you'd otherwise have
earned by renting or selling it. Not zero just because no money
changes hands.

**2. Include cannibalization (or growth) effects.** Apple launching
an iPhone replaces some iPod sales. The right incremental revenue
counts only what's _new_, net of erosion of existing products. If
the new product also boosts Mac sales (the "halo" effect), include
that too.

**3. Exclude sunk costs.** You spent \$2M on market research before
deciding whether to launch. That \$2M is gone whether you launch or
not. Do not include it in NPV. The decision is forward-looking;
sunk costs are backward-looking.

**4. Exclude financing cash flows.** Interest, principal, and
dividends are not in project FCF. Interest-bearing financing affects the
cost of capital; principal repayments and dividends are distributions to
capital providers, not inputs to the discount rate. Keeping all three out
of project FCF separates the operating project from how it is financed and
avoids double-counting financing effects.

**5. Allocate overhead carefully.** A new product line is often
charged a share of corporate overhead (HR, the head office, existing
IT). Include only the overhead that actually _rises_ because of the
project. Costs the firm would incur anyway are not incremental, even
when the accountants assign a slice of them to the project.

## Working capital — the hidden cost of growth

Growing firms tie up cash in working capital even when they're
profitable. As an **illustrative calibration**, the simulation allows a
project to require \$0.10-\$0.20 of working capital per revenue dollar
(accounts receivable + inventory − accounts payable). The right ratio is
industry- and business-model-specific and should come from comparable
firms or the project's operating plan. For a fast-growing project:

- ΔAR rises with sales (booked revenue not yet collected).
- ΔInventory rises with sales (cash spent on product not yet sold).
- ΔAP rises with COGS (cash owed to suppliers but not yet paid).
- ΔWC = ΔAR + ΔInventory − ΔAP.

When ΔWC > 0, it _subtracts_ from FCF. This is why a profitable
growing company can still need outside financing — the income
statement says everything is fine, the cash flow statement says you
need money.

Reverse direction: when a project winds down, working capital is
_released_ (collected receivables, sold inventory). Don't forget
this at the project's terminal year — it's real cash.

## Terminal value

Most projects don't have a natural end. Two common ways to capture
value beyond the forecast horizon:

**Gordon growth (perpetuity).** Assume year $T+1$ cash flow grows
forever at rate $g$, with discount rate $r$:

$$
TV_T = \frac{FCF_{T+1}}{r - g}.
$$

The formula requires $r>g$; otherwise the growing perpetuity does not
have a finite positive value. The cash flow, $r$, and $g$ must also share
the same nominal or real basis. The total PV is then the explicit forecast
plus $TV_T / (1+r)^T$. Be conservative with $g$: a real-cash-flow model
might use a low single-digit real growth rate, whereas a nominal model
must pair nominal growth with a nominal discount rate. In either case,
terminal growth should not outpace the broader economy indefinitely.

**Exit multiple.** Assume the project (or business) sells in year
$T$ for some multiple of its EBITDA or EBIT. Used heavily in
private-equity and M&A valuations:

$$
TV_T = \text{Exit multiple} \times \text{EBITDA}_T.
$$

Both methods are sensitive to assumptions. Sensitivity-test them
both (next lesson).

## A worked summary

Suppose a continuing project has an 8-year explicit forecast, year-1
revenue of \$1,000, 5% annual growth, a 30% gross margin, cash operating
expenses equal to 12% of sales, a 25% tax rate, working capital equal to
10% of sales, and \$800 of initial CapEx with 8-year straight-line
depreciation. Annual sustaining CapEx is \$100, matching the original
asset's annual D&A in this simplified forecast. The discount rate is 10%,
and terminal value uses $g = 2\%$ with continuing working-capital and
fixed-asset reinvestment.

In year 1, COGS is \$700 and cash operating expenses are \$120, so
EBITDA is $1{,}000 - 700 - 120 = \$180$. D&A is \$100, EBIT is \$80,
and NOPAT is $80(1 - 0.25) = \$60$. Because the initial working-capital
investment is paid at $t=0$, year-1 FCF is
$60 + 100 - 100 = \$60$. In the terminal calculation, sustaining CapEx
continues and grows with the asset base; setting it to zero would describe
an asset-light operation or produce an optimistic continuing value.
