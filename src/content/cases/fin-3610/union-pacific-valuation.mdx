## The question

On January 30, 2026, a Union Pacific share closed at \$235.10. What is
a share worth if you count only the cash the railroad's operations can
pay out, discounted at the
[7.57% cost of capital](/cases/fin-3610/union-pacific-cost-of-capital)
from the previous case?

The method is the one in
[Valuing Stocks](/lessons/fin-3610/valuing-stocks): forecast free
cash flow, add a terminal value, discount at the WACC, and subtract net
debt. This case builds the forecast from the railroad's own five-year
record, then asks how much the answer depends on the guesses.

## Five years of cash flow

The lesson
[Capital Budgeting: Incremental Free Cash Flows](/lessons/fin-3610/capital-budgeting-cashflows)
defines free cash flow as

$$
\text{FCF} = \text{EBIT} \times (1 - t) + \text{depreciation} - \Delta \text{working capital} - \text{capital investments}.
$$

EBIT is operating income. The tax rate $t$ is 24.1%, the rate on the
next dollar of income from the previous case. Revenue, operating income,
depreciation, capital investments and the working capital lines come
from Union Pacific's annual reports. The margin, working capital
investment and free cash flow columns are computed from them. Amounts
are in millions of dollars.

| Year | Revenue | Operating income | Operating margin | Depreciation | Capital investments | Working capital investment | Free cash flow |
| ---- | ------- | ---------------- | ---------------- | ------------ | ------------------- | -------------------------- | -------------- |
| 2021 | 21,804  | 9,338            | 42.8%            | 2,208        | 2,936               | −203                       | 6,563          |
| 2022 | 24,875  | 9,917            | 39.9%            | 2,246        | 3,620               | −8                         | 6,161          |
| 2023 | 24,119  | 9,082            | 37.7%            | 2,318        | 3,606               | 303                        | 5,302          |
| 2024 | 24,250  | 9,713            | 40.1%            | 2,398        | 3,452               | −186                       | 6,504          |
| 2025 | 24,510  | 9,846            | 40.2%            | 2,465        | 3,791               | 361                        | 5,786          |

Working capital investment is the sum of the five "changes in current
assets and liabilities" lines of the cash flow statement, with the sign
reversed: a positive number used cash. Railroads report the margin as an
**operating ratio**, operating expenses over revenue. Union Pacific's
was 59.8% in 2025, which is a margin of 40.2%.

The 10-K also reports a "free cash flow" of its own, defined
differently: cash from operations less investing and dividends, \$2,292
million in 2025. This case uses the lesson's definition: the cash the
operations produce for all investors, before any of it is paid to them
as interest or dividends.

**By hand, 2025.**

$$
9{,}846 \times (1 - 0.241) + 2{,}465 - 361 - 3{,}791 = 7{,}473 + 2{,}465 - 361 - 3{,}791 = 5{,}786.
$$

Over the five years together, the railroad reinvested \$6,037 million
of its \$36,353 million of after-tax operating income: a **reinvestment
share** of 16.6%. The valuing stocks lesson calls this the retention
rate $b$. Revenue grew from 21,804 to 24,510, which is 2.97% a year
compounded over the four years. Nearly all of that came in 2022; from
2022 to 2025 revenue slipped about 0.5% a year. The margin averaged
40.1% and ranged from 37.7% to 42.8%.

## The forecast

Three numbers drive it:

- **Revenue growth, years 1 to 5: 3%.** The 2021 to 2025 rate, rounded.
- **Operating margin: 40%.** The five-year average, rounded.
- **Long-run growth after year 5: 3%.** This case's assumption: the
  same as revenue growth in years 1 to 5, and below the 7.57% cost of
  capital, as the terminal value formula requires.

The reinvestment share stays at 16.6% and the tax rate at 24.1%, so
each year

$$
\text{FCF}_t = \text{revenue}_t \times 0.40 \times (1 - 0.241) \times (1 - 0.166).
$$

**By hand, year 1.** Revenue is 24,510 × 1.03 = 25,245, so free cash
flow is 25,245 × 0.40 × 0.759 × 0.834 = 6,392.

| Year | Revenue | Free cash flow | Present value at 7.57% |
| ---- | ------- | -------------- | ---------------------- |
| 2026 | 25,245  | 6,392          | 5,942                  |
| 2027 | 26,003  | 6,584          | 5,690                  |
| 2028 | 26,783  | 6,781          | 5,448                  |
| 2029 | 27,586  | 6,985          | 5,217                  |
| 2030 | 28,414  | 7,194          | 4,995                  |
| Sum  |         |                | 27,292                 |

Every figure is computed before rounding; working from the rounded
numbers can move the last digit by one.

The terminal value at the end of 2030 is the value of every later cash
flow:

$$
\text{TV} = \frac{\text{FCF}_{2030} \times 1.03}{0.0757 - 0.03} = \frac{7{,}194 \times 1.03}{0.0457} = 162{,}140,
$$

worth $162{,}140 / 1.0757^5 = 112{,}573$ today. Carried without
rounding, the terminal value is 162,150 and its present value 112,580,
the figure used below.

## From the firm to one share

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Enterprise value} &= 27{,}292 + 112{,}580 = 139{,}872 \\
\text{Net debt} &= 26{,}500 - 1{,}266 - 250 = 24{,}984 \\
\text{Equity value} &= 139{,}872 - 24{,}984 = 114{,}888 \\
\text{Per share} &= 114{,}888 / 593.4 = 193.61
\end{aligned}
$$

Net debt is the fair value of the debt, \$26,500 million, less cash of
\$1,266 million and short-term investments of \$250 million, all at
December 31, 2025. As in the previous case, debt is at fair value: what
the bonds would have cost to buy back on that date.

- **The terminal value's present value is 80% of the enterprise
  value** (112,580 of 139,872). What happens after 2030 matters more
  than the five years you forecast.
- **The answer is \$193.61, and the price was \$235.10.** The model's
  value is about 18% below the price.

## What was the price assuming?

Turn the question around. Hold every input at the base case except one,
and find the value of that one which makes the model equal \$235.10:

| Input changed         | Base case | Value that matches the price |
| --------------------- | --------- | ---------------------------- |
| Long-run growth       | 3.00%     | 3.79%                        |
| Operating margin      | 40.0%     | 47.0%                        |
| Cost of capital, WACC | 7.57%     | 6.89%                        |

A 47% margin is about 4 points above the best year in the table (42.8%
in 2021). A 6.89% WACC is below the low end of every range in the
previous case, where the lowest was 7.32%.

Is 3.79% growth forever plausible? The valuing stocks lesson ties
growth to reinvestment: growth = $b$ × return on new investment. With
$b$ = 16.6%, even the base case's 3% needs a return of 3 / 16.6 =
18.1%, and the price's 3.79% needs 3.79 / 16.6 = 22.8%. Union Pacific's
report puts its return on invested capital, as adjusted (a measure the
company defines), at 16.3% in 2025: after-tax operating income over all
the capital already in the business, at book value. That is an
average, not the return on the next project, so treat it as a
yardstick. By that yardstick the price needs new projects far more
profitable than the railroad's average, or more reinvestment, which
lowers free cash flow.

There are other readings of the gap. Investors may expect margins to
keep improving. The price may also reflect the merger with Norfolk
Southern, then pending, which this valuation of Union Pacific alone
leaves out. The case teaches a method; it is not a view on whether to
buy or sell the stock.

## Simulate it

A single number hides how uncertain it is. The chart below uses the
Monte Carlo method from the
[sensitivity lesson](/lessons/fin-3610/sensitivity-and-scenario): it
draws 2,000 random scenarios. Each draw picks one revenue growth rate,
one margin and one long-run growth rate, then values the share.

You set three numbers for each input: low, most likely and high. Each
input comes from a **triangular** distribution: never below low or
above high, most often near the most likely value, with the chance
falling in a straight line toward each end. Its average is (low + most
likely + high) / 3, so the revenue range (0, 3, 5) averages 2.67%.

<DcfMonteCarlo client:visible />

Work through these steps.

1. Read the readouts. With every input at its most likely value the
   share is worth \$193.61. The middle of the 2,000 draws is \$190.45.
   The middle 80% (10% of draws fall below it and 10% above) runs from
   \$164.94 to \$220.81. Of the draws, 3.5% reach the market price.
2. Press **Draw again** a few times. The middle moves by a dollar or so,
   and the share of draws at or above the price stays near 3% to 4%.
3. Press **Reset**. Then, in **Long-run growth after year 5**, drag low
   up to 3.0% and high down to 3.0%. The middle 80% narrows to \$173.47
   to \$206.59, and no draw reaches the price: the best possible draw is
   now \$234.75.
4. Press **Reset**, then drag long-run growth high to 5.0%. Now 22.6%
   of the draws reach the price. (5% forever would outgrow the economy,
   so treat this as a stress test.)
5. Press **Reset**, then drag long-run growth most likely to 3.8%. The
   most likely case is worth \$235.66, about the price.

## What the draws show

**Of the three inputs drawn, long-run growth drives the spread.**
Fixing one input at its most likely value and drawing the other two:

| Input fixed at most likely | Middle 80% of values | Width |
| -------------------------- | -------------------- | ----- |
| None (all three drawn)     | \$164.94 to \$220.81 | 55.87 |
| Revenue growth             | \$170.58 to \$221.85 | 51.27 |
| Operating margin           | \$166.25 to \$219.84 | 53.59 |
| Long-run growth            | \$173.47 to \$206.59 | 33.12 |

Removing the uncertainty in long-run growth narrows the range by \$22.75.
Removing either of the others narrows it by less than \$5. Spreads do
not add up: independent uncertainties partly offset, so a smaller one
adds little once a larger one is present. On its own, the margin range
still spans about \$20 of value. Long-run growth matters most because
the terminal value is 80% of the value and long-run growth sits in its
denominator.

**The middle is below the most likely case.** The revenue growth range
runs from 0% to 5% around 3%, so 60% of its draws fall below 3%. That
pulls the middle down. Long-run growth pulls the other way: a point more
adds more value than a point less takes away (\$249.12 at 4% and
\$158.04 at 2%, against \$193.61 at 3%), which stretches the right tail.
Here the first effect wins, and the middle ends at \$190.45.

**The ranges are a choice.** The draws are only as good as the ranges.
Moving one end of one range, as in step 4, moved the share of draws at
or above the price from 3.5% to 22.6%.

## Takeaways

- Free cash flow is operating income after tax less what the firm
  reinvests. For Union Pacific that was about 83% of after-tax
  operating income from 2021 to 2025.
- Most of the value sits in the terminal value, so long-run growth and
  the cost of capital matter most. Spend your judgment there.
- Asking what the price assumes turns one uncertain answer into a
  question you can check: is 3.79% growth forever believable?

## Limits

- One revenue growth rate covers all five years, and the three draws
  are independent. In a recession, growth and margins fall together,
  which would widen both ends of the range.
- The reinvestment share stays at 16.6% whatever the growth rate, so in
  the model faster growth costs nothing. The valuing stocks lesson says
  it does: at a given return on new investment, more growth needs more
  reinvestment and leaves less free cash flow. Holding the share fixed
  makes high-growth draws too valuable and low-growth draws too cheap.
  If the return on new investment were held at the base case's 18.1%
  instead, 2% to 4% long-run growth would move the value from \$168.27
  to \$232.85, not \$158.04 to \$249.12, and the price would imply 4.04%
  growth.
- The cost of capital stays at 7.57% in every draw. At the previous
  case's 9.75% (with the historical premium) the base case is worth
  \$117.49 a share.
- Taxes are 24.1% of operating income. At the firm's 2025 effective
  rate of 22.1% the value would be about \$201.
- Lease payments are inside operating income, so lease liabilities stay
  out of net debt. Pension obligations are left out.
- The value is at December 31, 2025, and the price is from January 30, 2026. Rolling the value forward a month at 7.57% adds about \$1.44 a
  share.
- The margin range spans 2021 to 2025 (37.7% to 42.8%, rounded
  outward). The two growth ranges are this case's judgments, not
  estimates from data.
