## The question

On January 30, 2026, a Union Pacific share closed at \$235.10. What is
a share worth if you count only the cash the railroad's operations can
pay out, discounted at the
[7.57% cost of capital](/cases/fin-3610/union-pacific-cost-of-capital)
from the previous case?

The method is the one in
[Valuing Stocks](/lessons/fin-3610/valuing-stocks): forecast free
cash flow, add a terminal value, discount at the WACC, and subtract net
debt. This case builds the forecast from the railroad's own five-year
record, then asks how much the answer depends on the guesses.

## Five years of cash flow

The lesson
[Capital Budgeting: Incremental Free Cash Flows](/lessons/fin-3610/capital-budgeting-cashflows)
defines free cash flow as

$$
\text{FCF} = \text{EBIT} \times (1 - t) + \text{depreciation} - \Delta \text{working capital} - \text{capital investments}.
$$

EBIT is operating income. The tax rate $t$ is 24.1%, the rate on the
next dollar of income from the previous case. Every other number comes
from Union Pacific's annual reports. Amounts are in millions of dollars.

| Year | Revenue | Operating income | Operating margin | Depreciation | Capital investments | Working capital investment | Free cash flow |
| ---- | ------- | ---------------- | ---------------- | ------------ | ------------------- | -------------------------- | -------------- |
| 2021 | 21,804  | 9,338            | 42.8%            | 2,208        | 2,936               | −203                       | 6,563          |
| 2022 | 24,875  | 9,917            | 39.9%            | 2,246        | 3,620               | −8                         | 6,161          |
| 2023 | 24,119  | 9,082            | 37.7%            | 2,318        | 3,606               | 303                        | 5,302          |
| 2024 | 24,250  | 9,713            | 40.1%            | 2,398        | 3,452               | −186                       | 6,504          |
| 2025 | 24,510  | 9,846            | 40.2%            | 2,465        | 3,791               | 361                        | 5,786          |

Working capital investment is the five "changes in current assets and
liabilities" lines of the cash flow statement with the sign reversed: a
positive number used cash. Railroads report the same margin as an
**operating ratio**, operating expenses over revenue. Union Pacific's
was 59.8% in 2025, which is a margin of 40.2%.

**By hand, 2025.**

$$
9{,}846 \times (1 - 0.241) + 2{,}465 - 361 - 3{,}791 = 7{,}473 + 2{,}465 - 361 - 3{,}791 = 5{,}786.
$$

Over the five years together, the railroad reinvested 6,037 of its
36,353 million dollars of after-tax operating income: a **reinvestment
share** of 16.6%. Revenue grew from 21,804 to 24,510, which is 2.97% a
year compounded. The margin averaged 40.1% and ranged from 37.7% to
42.8%.

## The forecast

Three numbers drive it:

- **Revenue growth, years 1 to 5: 3%.** The 2021 to 2025 rate, rounded.
- **Operating margin: 40%.** The five-year average, rounded.
- **Long-run growth after year 5: 3%.** This case's assumption.

The reinvestment share stays at 16.6% and the tax rate at 24.1%, so
each year

$$
\text{FCF}_t = \text{revenue}_t \times 0.40 \times (1 - 0.241) \times (1 - 0.166).
$$

**By hand, year 1.** Revenue is 24,510 × 1.03 = 25,245, so free cash
flow is 25,245 × 0.40 × 0.759 × 0.834 = 6,392.

| Year | Revenue | Free cash flow | Present value at 7.57% |
| ---- | ------- | -------------- | ---------------------- |
| 2026 | 25,245  | 6,392          | 5,942                  |
| 2027 | 26,003  | 6,584          | 5,690                  |
| 2028 | 26,783  | 6,781          | 5,448                  |
| 2029 | 27,586  | 6,985          | 5,217                  |
| 2030 | 28,414  | 7,194          | 4,995                  |
| Sum  |         |                | 27,292                 |

The terminal value at the end of 2030 is the value of every later cash
flow:

$$
\text{TV} = \frac{\text{FCF}_{2030} \times 1.03}{0.0757 - 0.03} = \frac{7{,}194 \times 1.03}{0.0457} = 162{,}140,
$$

worth $162{,}140 / 1.0757^5 = 112{,}574$ today. (The code keeps more
decimals and gets 112,580; the difference is rounding.)

## From the firm to one share

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Enterprise value} &= 27{,}292 + 112{,}580 = 139{,}872 \\
\text{Net debt} &= 26{,}500 - 1{,}266 - 250 = 24{,}984 \\
\text{Equity value} &= 139{,}872 - 24{,}984 = 114{,}888 \\
\text{Per share} &= 114{,}888 / 593.4 = 193.61
\end{aligned}
$$

Net debt is the fair value of the debt, \$26,500 million, less cash of
\$1,266 million and short-term investments of \$250 million, all at
December 31, 2025. Fair value, as in the previous case: it is what the
bonds would cost to buy back today.

Two things stand out.

- **The terminal value is 80% of the enterprise value** (112,580 of
  139,872). What happens after 2030 matters more than the five years
  you forecast.
- **The answer is \$193.61, and the price was \$235.10.** The model puts
  the shares about 18% below what the market paid.

## What was the price assuming?

Turn the question around. Hold every input at the base case except one,
and find the value of that one which makes the model equal \$235.10:

| Input changed         | Base case | Value that matches the price |
| --------------------- | --------- | ---------------------------- |
| Long-run growth       | 3.00%     | 3.79%                        |
| Operating margin      | 40.0%     | 47.0%                        |
| Cost of capital, WACC | 7.57%     | 6.89%                        |

Is 3.79% growth forever plausible? The valuing stocks lesson ties
growth to reinvestment: growth = reinvestment share × return on new
investment. With 16.6% reinvested, 3.79% growth needs new investment to
earn 3.79 / 16.6 = 22.8%. Union Pacific's own report puts its return on
invested capital at 16.3% in 2025. Either new projects must earn far
more than the existing railroad, or the railroad must reinvest more,
which lowers free cash flow.

There are other readings of the gap. Investors may expect margins to
keep improving. The price may also reflect the pending merger with
Norfolk Southern, which this valuation of Union Pacific alone leaves
out. The case teaches a method; it is not a view on whether to buy or
sell the stock.

## Simulate it

A single number hides how uncertain it is. The chart below draws 2,000
possible futures. Each draw picks one revenue growth rate, one margin
and one long-run growth rate, then values the share. You set three
numbers for each input: low, most likely and high. A draw is most often
near the most likely value and less often near the ends (a
**triangular** distribution).

<DcfMonteCarlo client:visible />

Work through these steps.

1. Read the readouts. With every input at its most likely value the
   share is worth \$193.61. The middle of the 2,000 draws is \$190.45,
   and the middle 80% runs from \$164.94 to \$220.81. Only 3.5% of the
   draws reach the market price.
2. Press **Draw again** a few times. The middle moves by a dollar or so,
   and the share of draws at or above the price stays near 3% to 4%.
3. In **Long-run growth after year 5**, drag low up to 3.0% and high
   down to 3.0%. The middle 80% narrows to \$173.47 to \$206.59, and no
   draw reaches the price.
4. Press **Reset**, then drag long-run growth high to 5.0%. Now 22.6%
   of the draws reach the price.
5. Press **Reset**, then drag long-run growth most likely to 3.8%. The
   most likely case is worth \$235.66, about the price.

## What the draws show

**Long-run growth drives the spread.** Fixing one input at its most
likely value and drawing the other two:

| Input fixed at most likely | Middle 80% of values | Width |
| -------------------------- | -------------------- | ----- |
| None (all three drawn)     | \$164.94 to \$220.81 | 55.86 |
| Revenue growth             | \$170.58 to \$221.85 | 51.27 |
| Operating margin           | \$166.25 to \$219.84 | 53.59 |
| Long-run growth            | \$173.47 to \$206.59 | 33.13 |

Removing the uncertainty in long-run growth narrows the range by \$22.73.
Removing either of the others narrows it by less than \$5. That is the
terminal value at work: it is 80% of the value, and long-run growth
sits in its denominator.

**The middle is below the most likely case.** The revenue growth range
runs from 0% to 5% around 3%, so it has more room below 3% than above.
Its draws average 2.67%, which pulls the middle down to \$190.45.

**Wider ranges are a choice.** The draws are only as good as the
ranges. Moving one end of one range, as in step 4, moved the share of
draws at or above the price from 3.5% to 22.6%.

## Takeaways

- Free cash flow is operating income after tax less what the firm
  reinvests. For Union Pacific that was about 83% of after-tax
  operating income from 2021 to 2025.
- Most of the value sits in the terminal value, so long-run growth and
  the cost of capital matter most. Spend your judgment there.
- Turning the model around ("what does the price assume?") is often
  more useful than the model's own answer.

## Limits

- One revenue growth rate covers all five years, and the three draws
  are independent. In a recession, growth and margins fall together,
  which would widen the low end.
- The reinvestment share stays at 16.6% whatever the growth rate. The
  valuing stocks lesson says faster growth needs more reinvestment.
- Taxes are 24.1% of operating income. The firm's effective rate was
  22.1% in 2025.
- Net debt leaves out operating leases and pension obligations.
- The value is at December 31, 2025, and the price is from January 30, 2026.
- The ranges for the draws are this case's judgments, not estimates
  from data.
