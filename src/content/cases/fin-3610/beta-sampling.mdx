## The question

The CAPM gives a cost of equity from three numbers:

$$
r_E = r_f + \beta \times \text{MRP}.
$$

Two of them, the risk-free rate and the market risk premium (MRP), are
the same for every firm. Beta is the one an analyst estimates from the
firm's own past returns, usually the last five years of monthly returns.
How much would the answer change with a different five years?

This case estimates beta for two industries: utilities, whose stocks
move little with the market, and high tech, whose stocks move a lot. The
returns are real: the value-weighted utilities and high-tech portfolios
from the Ken French Data Library, and the market, each month from July
1963 to September 2026. Each industry is a portfolio of many firms, so
its beta is steadier than any one firm's.

## The regression

Beta is the slope of a regression of the industry's monthly return over
Treasury bills on the market's, as in
[Estimating Alpha and Beta](/lessons/fin-3610/factor-regressions):

$$
R_{i,t} - R_{f,t} = \alpha + \beta\,(R_{m,t} - R_{f,t}) + \varepsilon_t .
$$

The regression also reports a standard error for beta: how far the
estimate would typically land from the true beta if the same industry
were measured over another 60 months like these.

## By hand: high tech in 1999 and in 2004

An analyst in December 1999 regresses the 60 months from January 1995
to December 1999 and finds a beta of 1.435 with a standard error of
0.139. Five years later the next 60 months, January 2000 to December
2004, give 1.958 with a standard error of 0.118.

Hold the risk-free rate and premium at the values from
[Union Pacific's Cost of Capital](/cases/fin-3610/union-pacific-cost-of-capital),
4.26% and 4.23%, so that only beta changes:

$$
\begin{aligned}
r_E(1999) &= 4.26\% + 1.435 \times 4.23\% = 10.33\%, \\
r_E(2004) &= 4.26\% + 1.958 \times 4.23\% = 12.54\%.
\end{aligned}
$$

The same industry costs 2.21 percentage points more in equity. For a
payment of 100 a year forever, that is the difference between a value
of 100 / 0.1033 = 968 and 100 / 0.1254 = 797, about 18% less.

Is the gap just noise? A rough 95% range is the estimate plus or minus
two standard errors:

- 1999: 1.435 ± 2 × 0.139, from 1.157 to 1.713.
- 2004: 1.958 ± 2 × 0.118, from 1.722 to 2.194.

The two ranges do not overlap. Noise alone rarely puts two estimates
this far apart.

## Two reasons a beta moves

**Sampling error.** Sixty months is a small sample. Even if an industry's
true beta never changed, a different 60 months would give a different
estimate. The random draws measure this: they mix months from every
decade, so the true beta behind them is the full-history one, and the
middle 90% of their estimates still spans 0.37 for high tech.

**The beta itself changes.** If sampling error were the whole story,
about one 60-month window in ten would land outside the middle 90% of
the random draws. For high tech, 28% of the 700 windows do; for
utilities, 25%. The windows overlap, so they are not 700 separate tests,
but the middle chart shows the pattern: high tech's beta climbed to
about 2 in the windows ending in 2004 and was back near 1 by 2021. A
firm's or an industry's business, leverage and investors change, and
its beta changes with them.

So a longer window has two effects. More months cut the standard error,
roughly by the square root of the number of months: 240 months instead
of 60 halve it. But the older months describe the industry as it was,
not as it is.

## What analysts do about it

- **Report a range.** Over 1995 to 1999, beta from 1.16 to 1.71 means a
  cost of equity from 9.15% to 11.51%.
- **Average across firms.** An industry beta, as in the cost of capital
  case, is less noisy than one firm's. A single stock's beta has a much
  larger standard error than these portfolios'.
- **Pull extreme estimates toward 1.** Some data services report an
  adjusted beta, two thirds of the estimate plus one third of 1, because
  betas far from 1 tend to move back toward it.

## Takeaways

- A beta from 60 months is an estimate with a standard error. For high
  tech in 1995 to 1999 it was 0.139, enough to move the cost of equity by
  more than two points across a 95% range.
- Betas also change over time. High tech's ranged from about 1 to about
  2 across the 700 windows since 1968.
- Before using a beta, ask which months it came from and how precise it
  is.

## Limits

- These are industry portfolios. One firm's beta is noisier, and the
  firm can change faster than an industry.
- The random draws treat months as interchangeable. Real returns have
  calm and turbulent stretches, which the draws mix together, so they
  can understate how far apart two 60-month windows land.
- The rough 95% range assumes the regression's standard error is right.
  It assumes the monthly errors have constant variance and are unrelated
  from month to month, which real returns do not quite satisfy.
- The risk-free rate and premium are held at January 2026 values. An
  analyst in 1999 or 2004 would have used the rate and premium of that
  year.
- The regressions measure returns over the one-month Treasury bill rate,
  while the cost of equity uses the 10-year Treasury yield, as in the
  cost of capital case. Both are common choices.
