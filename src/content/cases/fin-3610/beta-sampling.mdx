## The question

The capital asset pricing model (CAPM) gives a cost of equity from three
numbers:

$$
r_E = r_f + \beta \times \text{MRP}.
$$

Two of them, the risk-free rate and the market risk premium (MRP), are
the same for every firm. Beta is the one an analyst estimates from the
firm's own past returns, often the last five years of monthly returns.
How much would the answer change with a different five years?

This case estimates beta for two industries: utilities, whose stocks
move less than the market, and high tech, whose stocks move more. The
returns are historical: the utilities and high-tech portfolios from the
Ken French Data Library, and the market, each month from July 1963 to
September 2026. Each portfolio is value weighted, so bigger firms count
more. Each industry holds many firms, so its beta is estimated more
precisely than a single firm's.

## The regression

Beta is the slope of a regression of the industry's excess return, its
return minus the one-month Treasury bill rate, on the market's excess
return, as in
[Estimating Alpha and Beta](/lessons/fin-3610/factor-regressions):

$$
R_{i,t} - R_{f,t} = \alpha + \beta\,(R_{m,t} - R_{f,t}) + \varepsilon_t .
$$

The regression also reports a standard error for beta: how far the
estimate would typically land from the true beta if the same industry
were measured over another 60 months like these. A rough 95% confidence
interval is the estimate plus or minus two standard errors. Intervals
built this way contain the true beta in about 95% of samples.

## By hand: high tech in 1999 and in 2004

An analyst in December 1999 regresses the 60 months from January 1995
to December 1999 and finds a beta of 1.435 with a standard error of
0.139. Five years later, the next 60 months, January 2000 to December
2004, give 1.958 with a standard error of 0.118.

Hold the risk-free rate at 4.26%, the 10-year Treasury yield on 30
January 2026, and the premium at 4.23%, Aswath Damodaran's implied
premium for January 2026, so that only beta changes. The next case,
[Union Pacific's Cost of Capital](/cases/fin-3610/union-pacific-cost-of-capital),
explains both inputs.

$$
\begin{aligned}
r_E(1999) &= 4.26\% + 1.435 \times 4.23\% = 10.33\%, \\
r_E(2004) &= 4.26\% + 1.958 \times 4.23\% = 12.54\%.
\end{aligned}
$$

The same industry's cost of equity is 2.21 percentage points higher. For
a payment of 100 a year forever, the value is 100 / 0.1033 = 968 at the
lower rate and 100 / 0.1254 = 797 at the higher one, about 18% lower.

Is the gap just noise? The two rough 95% confidence intervals:

- 1999: 1.435 ± 2 × 0.139, from 1.157 to 1.713.
- 2004: 1.958 ± 2 × 0.118, from 1.722 to 2.194.

They do not overlap. A direct check: the gap is 1.958 − 1.435 = 0.523.
The two windows share no months, so the gap's standard error is
$\sqrt{0.139^2 + 0.118^2} = 0.182$. The gap is 2.9 standard errors, and
noise alone gives a gap that large less than once in 100 samples.

## Two reasons a beta moves

**Sampling error.** Sixty months is a small sample. Even if an industry's
true beta never changed, a different 60 months would give a different
estimate. The random draws measure this: they mix months from every
decade, so the true beta behind them is the full-history one, and the
middle 90% of their estimates still spans 0.37 for high tech. The middle
90% only shows the spread; it is not a test, which is why it is narrower
than the 95% intervals above.

**The beta itself changes.** If beta never changed and months were
interchangeable, as the draws assume, about one 60-month window in ten
would land outside the draws' middle 90%. For high tech, 28% to 34% of
the 700 windows do, depending on the draws; for utilities, about 25%.
Treat this as a hint, not a test. Neighboring windows share 59 of their
60 months, so they land outside together, and the 700 windows hold only
about a dozen separate five-year periods. Calm and turbulent stretches
also push some windows outside (see Limits). The middle chart shows the
pattern: high tech's beta climbed to about 2 in the windows ending in
2004 and was back near 1 by 2021. A firm's business and debt change, and
so does an industry portfolio's mix of firms; beta changes with them.

So a longer window has two effects. The standard error shrinks with the
square root of the number of months: four times as many months, 240
instead of 60, roughly halve it. But the older months describe the
industry as it was, not as it is.

## What analysts do about it

- **Report a range.** For high tech over 1995 to 1999, beta from 1.157
  to 1.713 means a cost of equity from 9.15% to 11.51%.
- **Average across firms.** An industry beta, as in the next case, is
  less noisy than one firm's. A single stock's beta has a much larger
  standard error than these portfolios'.
- **Check whether beta has moved.** Rolling betas, like the middle
  chart, show whether a recent estimate is typical. Some analysts use
  recent data at a higher frequency, such as two years of weekly
  returns.
- **Pull extreme estimates toward 1.** Some data services report an
  adjusted beta: two thirds of the estimate plus one third of 1. The
  weights round the ones Marshall Blume estimated in 1971, when he found
  that betas far from 1 tend to move back toward it in later years. The
  adjustment turns the 2004 estimate of 1.958 into 1.639 and shrinks the
  gap with 1999 from 0.523 to 0.349.

## Takeaways

- A beta from 60 months is an estimate with a standard error. For high
  tech in 1995 to 1999 it was 0.139, enough to move the cost of equity
  by more than two percentage points across a rough 95% confidence
  interval.
- Betas also change over time. High tech's ranged from about 1 to about
  2 across the 700 windows since 1968.
- Before using a beta, ask which months it came from and how precise it
  is.

## Limits

- These are industry portfolios. One firm's beta is noisier, and the
  firm can change faster than an industry.
- The random draws treat months as interchangeable. Actual returns have
  calm and turbulent stretches, which the draws mix together, so the
  draws can understate how far apart two 60-month windows land even
  with a constant beta.
- The rough 95% confidence interval assumes the regression's standard
  error is right. The standard error assumes the monthly errors have
  constant variance and are unrelated from month to month, which actual
  returns do not quite satisfy.
- The risk-free rate and premium are held at January 2026 values. An
  analyst in 1999 or 2004 would have used the rate and premium of that
  year.
- The regressions measure returns over the one-month Treasury bill rate,
  while the cost of equity uses the 10-year Treasury yield, as in the
  next case. Beta barely depends on which rate is subtracted, because
  the bill rate moves little from month to month next to stock returns.
