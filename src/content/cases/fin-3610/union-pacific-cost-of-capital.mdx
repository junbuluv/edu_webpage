## The question

On January 30, 2026, Union Pacific had 593.4 million shares trading at
\$235.10 and about \$32 billion of debt on its balance sheet. If the
railroad spends another dollar on track or locomotives, what return must
that dollar earn to be worth spending? For an investment of average
risk, the answer is the firm's **weighted average cost of capital**
(WACC).

The lesson gives the formulas. This case is about the inputs: where each
number comes from, and how far apart two careful analysts can land.

One note on timing. In July 2025 Union Pacific agreed to merge with
Norfolk Southern, and when this report was filed the deal was still
waiting for regulators. The case estimates the cost of capital of Union
Pacific as it stood.

## The formula

$$
r_{\text{wacc}} = \frac{E}{V}\, r_E + \frac{D}{V}\, r_D (1 - t),
\qquad
r_E = r_f + \beta \times \text{premium}.
$$

$E$ and $D$ are the market values of equity and debt, and $V = E + D$.
There are six inputs: the risk-free rate $r_f$, beta, the equity risk
premium, the cost of debt $r_D$, the tax rate $t$, and debt's share of
value $D/V$.

## Input by input

### The weights

Equity is worth what the stock market says. The annual report gives the
share count and the closing price on January 30, 2026:

$$
E = 593{,}391{,}460 \times 235.10 = 139.5 \text{ billion dollars.}
$$

Debt is on the balance sheet at \$31.8 billion. The report also gives
its **fair value**, what the same bonds would sell for today: \$26.5
billion. The bonds are worth less than their face value because
today's yields are higher than the rates those bonds pay. Use the fair
value:

$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= 139.5 + 26.5 = 166.0 \\
\frac{D}{V} &= \frac{26.5}{166.0} = 15.96\%, \qquad \frac{E}{V} = 84.04\%.
\end{aligned}
$$

**The trap.** The balance sheet shows debt of \$31.8 billion and
shareholders' equity of \$18.5 billion, which makes debt 63% of the
total. Book equity is roughly what shareholders paid in plus the profits
the firm kept, minus buybacks. It is not what the shares are worth
today. Weights come from market values.

### The cost of equity

- **Risk-free rate, 4.26%.** The 10-year Treasury yield on January 30, 2026.
- **Beta, 0.98.** The average for the four US railroads in Aswath
  Damodaran's January 2026 table. An industry average is steadier than
  one firm's estimate, because the errors in the single estimates partly
  cancel.
- **Equity risk premium, 4.23%.** Damodaran's implied premium at the
  start of 2026: the premium at which the present value of the stock
  market's expected cash flows equals its price.

$$
r_E = 4.26\% + 0.98 \times 4.23\% = 8.405\%.
$$

### The cost of debt

The report says Union Pacific paid an effective interest rate of 4.1% on
its debt in 2025. That is the cost of bonds sold in the past. The cost
of capital is about the next dollar, and new borrowing costs more: in
January 2026 Moody's index of long-term Aaa corporate bonds yielded
5.34% and its Baa index 5.88%. The report lists a fall "below investment
grade" among its risks, so the debt is investment grade today. That band
runs from Aaa down to Baa, so take the midpoint of the two indexes:

$$
r_D = \frac{5.34\% + 5.88\%}{2} = 5.61\%.
$$

Interest is deducted before tax. The rate that matters is the one on the
next dollar of income: 21.0% federal plus 3.1% state, 24.1% in all. (The
firm's effective rate for 2025, taxes paid over pre-tax income, was
22.1%.)

$$
r_D (1 - t) = 5.61\% \times (1 - 0.241) = 4.258\%.
$$

### Putting it together

$$
\begin{aligned}
r_{\text{wacc}} &= 0.8404 \times 8.405\% + 0.1596 \times 4.258\% \\
&= 7.06\% + 0.68\% = 7.74\%.
\end{aligned}
$$

## How far apart do reasonable analysts land?

Move one input at a time across the range a careful analyst might use,
and hold the other five at the estimate:

| Input               | Low    | High   | WACC at low | WACC at high | Swing (points) |
| ------------------- | ------ | ------ | ----------- | ------------ | -------------- |
| Equity risk premium | 4.23%  | 7.03%  | 7.74%       | 10.05%       | 2.31           |
| Beta                | 0.86   | 1.10   | 7.32%       | 8.17%        | 0.85           |
| Risk-free rate      | 4.18%  | 4.87%  | 7.68%       | 8.26%        | 0.58           |
| Debt share          | 15.96% | 21.75% | 7.74%       | 7.50%        | 0.24           |
| Cost of debt        | 4.10%  | 5.88%  | 7.56%       | 7.78%        | 0.22           |
| Tax rate            | 21.0%  | 24.1%  | 7.77%       | 7.74%        | 0.03           |

Where the ranges come from:

- **Premium.** The low end is the implied premium. The high end is the
  1928 to 2025 average of the stock market's return over the 10-year
  Treasury bond's: 11.86% minus 4.82%.
- **Beta.** The low end is Damodaran's beta for all transportation
  firms. The high end is an assumption: the same distance above 0.98.
- **Risk-free rate.** The 10-year yield at year-end 2025, and the
  30-year yield on January 30, 2026.
- **Debt share.** Union Pacific's own, and the railroad industry's
  average.
- **Cost of debt.** What Union Pacific pays on its existing bonds, and
  the Baa yield.
- **Tax rate.** Federal only, and federal plus state.

**One input does most of the work.** The premium moves the WACC by 2.31
points, more than the other five together (1.92). Both ends of its range
are defensible: one reads today's prices, the other averages 98 years of
history.

**It changes what things are worth.** \$100 a year forever is worth
\$1,291 at 7.74% and \$995 at 10.05%, which is 23% less. Two analysts
who agree on every cash flow can disagree by a quarter of the value.

**Debt barely matters here.** Debt is 16% of Union Pacific's value, so
the cost of debt and the tax rate together move the WACC by a quarter of
a point.

## Takeaways

- Weights come from market values. Book values put Union Pacific's debt
  at 63% of the firm when it is 16%.
- The cost of debt is the yield on new borrowing, 5.61% here, not the
  4.1% the firm pays on old bonds.
- Spend your effort on the premium and beta. The tax rate is worth 0.03
  points.

## Limits

- The beta is the average of four railroads, not Union Pacific's own.
  Estimating its own beta needs its monthly stock returns.
- A slider moves one input and holds the rest. In reality more debt
  makes the equity riskier and raises its cost, which is the subject of
  the capital structure unit.
- The cost of debt comes from indexes of bonds with 20 or more years to
  maturity, not from Union Pacific's own bond prices.
- The inputs are not all from one day: the share price is from January
  30, 2026, the debt from December 31, 2025, and the beta and premium
  tables from January 2026.
- This is the rate for an investment as risky as the railroad itself. A
  riskier project needs a higher rate.
