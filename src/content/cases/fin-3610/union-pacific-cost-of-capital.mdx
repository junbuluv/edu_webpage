## The question

On January 30, 2026, Union Pacific had 593.4 million shares trading at
\$235.10. Its balance sheet a month earlier showed about \$32 billion of
debt. If the railroad spends another dollar on track or locomotives,
what return must that dollar earn to be worth spending? For an
investment of average risk, the answer is the firm's **weighted average
cost of capital** (WACC).

The lesson [Estimating the Cost of Capital](/lessons/fin-3610/cost-of-capital)
gives the formulas. This case is about the inputs: where each number
comes from, and how far apart two careful analysts can land.

One note on timing. In July 2025 Union Pacific agreed to merge with
Norfolk Southern. When its 2025 annual report was filed in February 2026
the rail regulator had not approved the deal, and in January 2026 it had
sent the first application back as incomplete. The case estimates the
cost of capital of Union Pacific as it stood.

## The formula

$$
r_{\text{wacc}} = \frac{E}{V}\, r_E + \frac{D}{V}\, r_D (1 - t),
\qquad
r_E = r_f + \beta \times \text{premium}.
$$

$E$ and $D$ are the market values of equity and debt, and $V = E + D$.
There are six inputs: the risk-free rate $r_f$, beta, the equity risk
premium (the lessons call it the market risk premium), the cost of debt
$r_D$, the tax rate $t$, and debt's share of value $D/V$.

## Input by input

### The weights

Equity is worth what the stock market says. The annual report gives the
share count and the closing price on January 30, 2026:

$$
E = 593{,}391{,}460 \times 235.10 = 139.5 \text{ billion dollars.}
$$

Debt is on the balance sheet at \$31.8 billion. The report also gives
its **fair value**, what the same bonds would sell for today: \$26.5
billion. The bonds are worth less than the amount on the balance sheet
because today's yields are higher than the rates those bonds pay. Use
the fair value:

$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= 139.5 + 26.5 = 166.0 \\
\frac{D}{V} &= \frac{26.5}{166.0} = 15.96\%, \qquad \frac{E}{V} = 84.04\%.
\end{aligned}
$$

**The trap.** The balance sheet shows debt of \$31.8 billion and
shareholders' equity of \$18.5 billion, which makes debt 63% of the
total. Book equity is roughly what shareholders paid in plus the profits
the firm kept, minus buybacks. It is not what the shares are worth
today. Weights come from market values.

### The tax rate

Interest is deducted from income before tax is figured, so a dollar of
interest costs the firm $(1 - t)$ dollars. The rate that matters is the
one on the next dollar of income: 21.0% federal plus 3.1% state (after
the federal deduction), 24.1% in all. The firm's effective rate for
2025, income tax expense over pre-tax income, was 22.1%.

### The cost of equity

- **Risk-free rate, 4.26%.** The 10-year Treasury yield on January 30, 2026. The 10-year is the usual choice for long-lived investments, and
  it is the rate the premium below is measured against.
- **Beta, 0.93.** Estimating Union Pacific's own beta needs its monthly
  stock returns. The lesson's other route is to start from comparable
  firms. Aswath Damodaran's January 2026 table gives the four US
  railroads an unlevered beta of 0.81. Re-lever it at Union Pacific's
  own debt-to-equity ratio, $26.5 / 139.5 = 0.19$:

$$
\beta = 0.81 \times \left[1 + (1 - 0.241) \times 0.19\right] = 0.93.
$$

- **Equity risk premium, 4.23%.** Damodaran's implied premium at the
  start of 2026: the discount rate at which the present value of the
  S&P 500's expected dividends and buybacks equals its price, minus the
  10-year Treasury yield. It reads today's prices instead of past
  returns. It is also the lowest of the common choices: the lesson's
  working figure of 5.5% would give a WACC of 8.56%.

$$
r_E = 4.26\% + 0.93 \times 4.23\% = 8.194\%.
$$

### The cost of debt

The report says Union Pacific's effective interest rate (interest
expense over average debt) was 4.1% in 2025. That is the cost of bonds
sold in the past. The cost of capital is about the next dollar, and new
borrowing costs more: in January 2026 Moody's index of long-term Aaa
corporate bonds yielded 5.34% and its Baa index 5.88%. The report lists
a fall "below investment grade" among its risks, so the debt is
investment grade today, somewhere between Aaa and Baa. It does not name
the rating, so as a rough stand-in take the midpoint of the two indexes:

$$
r_D = \frac{5.34\% + 5.88\%}{2} = 5.61\%, \qquad r_D (1 - t) = 5.61\% \times (1 - 0.241) = 4.258\%.
$$

### Putting it together

$$
\begin{aligned}
r_{\text{wacc}} &= 0.8404 \times 8.194\% + 0.1596 \times 4.258\% \\
&= 6.89\% + 0.68\% = 7.57\%.
\end{aligned}
$$

## How far apart do reasonable analysts land?

Move one input at a time across the range a careful analyst might use,
and hold the other five at the estimate:

| Input               | Low    | High   | WACC at low | WACC at high | Swing (percentage points) |
| ------------------- | ------ | ------ | ----------- | ------------ | ------------------------- |
| Equity risk premium | 4.23%  | 7.03%  | 7.57%       | 9.75%        | 2.19                      |
| Risk-free rate      | 4.18%  | 4.87%  | 7.50%       | 8.08%        | 0.58                      |
| Beta                | 0.86   | 0.98   | 7.32%       | 7.74%        | 0.43                      |
| Debt share          | 15.96% | 21.75% | 7.57%       | 7.34%        | 0.23                      |
| Cost of debt        | 5.34%  | 5.88%  | 7.53%       | 7.60%        | 0.07                      |
| Tax rate            | 21.0%  | 24.1%  | 7.59%       | 7.57%        | 0.03                      |

Swings are computed before rounding, so a few differ by 0.01 from the
difference of the two columns.

Where the ranges come from:

- **Premium.** The low end is the implied premium. The high end is the
  1928 to 2025 arithmetic average of the stock market's yearly return
  minus the 10-year Treasury bond's, from Damodaran's annual returns
  table: 7.03% (11.86% minus 4.82% before rounding). Bonds, not bills,
  to match the 10-year risk-free rate.
- **Risk-free rate.** The 10-year yield at year-end 2025, and the
  30-year yield on January 30, 2026.
- **Beta.** The low end is the beta of Damodaran's broader
  Transportation group (19 firms). The high end is his railroad average
  as published, 0.98, before adjusting for Union Pacific's lighter debt.
- **Debt share.** Union Pacific's own, and the railroad average in
  Damodaran's table. The WACC falls here only because weight moves to
  the cheaper source while both costs stay put. A firm that really
  borrowed more would see its cost of equity rise, so this row does not
  show that more debt lowers the WACC.
- **Cost of debt.** The Aaa yield and the Baa yield.
- **Tax rate.** Federal only, and federal plus state.

**One input does most of the work.** The premium moves the WACC by 2.19
points, more than the other five swings added up (1.34). Both ends of
its range are defensible: one reads today's prices, the other averages
98 years of history.

**It changes what things are worth.** \$100 a year forever is worth
\$1,322 at the estimate and \$1,025 with the historical premium (both
from the unrounded rates). Two analysts who agree on every cash flow can
disagree by more than a fifth of the value.

**The debt inputs barely move the estimate.** Debt is 16% of Union
Pacific's value, so the cost of debt and the tax rate together move the
WACC by a tenth of a point. That says the estimate is not sensitive to
them. It does not say borrowing is unimportant to the firm.

## Takeaways

- Weights come from market values. Book values put Union Pacific's debt
  at 63% of the firm when it is 16%.
- The cost of debt is the yield on new borrowing, 5.61% here, not the
  4.1% the firm pays on old bonds.
- Spend your effort on the premium. The tax rate is worth 0.03 points.

## Limits

- A slider moves one input and holds the rest. That is least realistic
  for the debt share. More debt makes the equity riskier and raises its
  cost, and enough debt raises the cost of debt too. The chart holds
  both fixed, so it overstates how far the WACC falls as the debt share
  rises. Whether more debt lowers the WACC at all is the subject of the
  capital structure unit.
- The beta comes from four railroads, not from Union Pacific's own
  returns. An average of four is steadier than one estimate, but it
  measures the typical railroad.
- The lesson's first choice for the cost of debt is the yield on the
  firm's own bonds. Those prices are not in the annual report, so the
  case uses indexes of bonds with 20 or more years to maturity.
- The inputs are not all from one day: the share price is from January
  30, 2026, the debt from December 31, 2025, and the beta and premium
  tables from January 2026.
- This is the rate for an investment as risky as the railroad itself. A
  riskier project needs a higher rate.
