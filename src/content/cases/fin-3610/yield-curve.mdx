## The question

At the end of July 2023 a 3-month US Treasury bill yielded 5.55% and a
10-year Treasury note yielded 3.97%. Lenders accepted a lower rate for
tying up money for ten years than for three months. That shape is called
an **inverted yield curve**, and it has a reputation as a recession
warning.

This case uses month-end Treasury yields from FRED, the data service of
the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, from January 2000 to October 2026.
It asks two questions. What has an inverted curve been followed by? And
when the curve implies a rate a year ahead, how close does it come?

## The data

Each month has six yields, one per maturity: 3 months, 1 year, 2 years,
5 years, 10 years and 30 years. Plot yield against maturity and you have
that month's **yield curve**. Two numbers summarize its slope:

- the **10-year minus 2-year gap**, also called the term spread, and
- the **10-year minus 3-month gap**.

A gap is measured in percentage points. When it is negative, the curve
is inverted by that measure. Four months show the range of shapes:

| Month         | 3-month | 2-year | 10-year | 10-year minus 2-year (points) |
| ------------- | ------- | ------ | ------- | ----------------------------- |
| June 2004     | 1.33%   | 2.70%  | 4.62%   | 1.92                          |
| February 2007 | 5.16%   | 4.65%  | 4.56%   | −0.09                         |
| December 2008 | 0.11%   | 0.76%  | 2.25%   | 1.49                          |
| July 2023     | 5.55%   | 4.88%  | 3.97%   | −0.91                         |

Recession months come from FRED's USREC series. It codes the dates set
by the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER) and starts each
recession in the month after the peak. Sources that count from the peak
month date the 2008 recession from December 2007, one month earlier than
this case does.

## What an inverted curve implies

Start with a choice every lender has. To lend for two years you can buy
a 2-year note at yield $y_2$. Or you can buy a 1-year bill at yield
$y_1$ and lend again for the second year at the 1-year rate of that day.
Call $f$ the second-year rate that makes the two routes pay the same:

$$
(1 + y_2)^2 = (1 + y_1)(1 + f)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
f = \frac{(1 + y_2)^2}{1 + y_1} - 1 .
$$

$f$ is the **implied forward rate**: the 1-year rate one year from now
that today's curve is consistent with.

**By hand, July 2023.** The 1-year yield was 5.37% and the 2-year
4.88%:

$$
f = \frac{1.0488^2}{1.0537} - 1 = \frac{1.09998}{1.0537} - 1 = 4.39\% .
$$

The curve implied that the 1-year rate would fall from 5.37% to 4.39% a
year later. Rates usually fall that far when the Federal Reserve cuts
them, and the Fed usually cuts when the economy weakens. That is why an
inverted curve is read as a recession warning: the bond market is
pricing in lower rates ahead.

The implied rate is not a pure forecast. Lenders also ask for a term
premium, extra yield for holding a longer bond, which pushes $y_2$ up
and $f$ with it. So $f$ sits above the rate lenders expect.

## How good was the warning?

Count the stretches in which the 10-year minus 2-year gap was negative
at month-end, and what followed.

| Inverted                                   | What followed                                 |
| ------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------- |
| February to November 2000                  | Recession, April to November 2001             |
| December 2005 to May 2007, on and off      | Recession, January 2008 to June 2009          |
| July 2022 to July 2024, 25 months in a row | No recession in the data through October 2026 |

**The lag is long and uneven.** The 2001 recession started 14 months
after the first inverted month. The 2008 recession started 25 months
after. The lesson's rule of thumb is 6 to 18 months, and only the 2001
recession fits it. A warning that arrives one to two years early is hard
to act on.

**The largest inversion has had no recession so far.** The 2022 to 2024
stretch was the longest in the data and the deepest: the gap reached
−1.06 points in June 2023. Through October 2026 no recession has been
dated after it. Rates can fall without one: the 3-month yield went from
5.41% in July 2024 to 4.37% in December 2024.

**The measure matters.** In 2019 the 10-year minus 2-year gap touched
0.00 at the end of August and never closed a month below zero. The
10-year minus 3-month gap was negative from May to September 2019, and
again in January and February 2020. A recession did follow, in March and
April 2020, but it began with the pandemic shutdowns, which no bond
yield could have foreseen.

Now check the forecasts themselves, in four months:

| Month         | 1-year yield | Implied a year ahead | Actual a year later |
| ------------- | ------------ | -------------------- | ------------------- |
| June 2004     | 2.09%        | 3.31%                | 3.45%               |
| February 2007 | 4.96%        | 4.34%                | 1.77%               |
| December 2021 | 0.39%        | 1.07%                | 4.73%               |
| July 2023     | 5.37%        | 4.39%                | 4.73%               |

In these four months the curve had the direction right every time and
the size right once, in June 2004. In February 2007 it implied a small
fall, and the 1-year yield dropped to 1.77% as the 2008 recession began.
In December 2021 it implied 1.07%, and a year later the 1-year yield was
4.73%. In July 2023 it implied a fall of 0.98 points, and the fall was
0.64.

Four chosen months are not a test. Across all 310 months with a known
outcome, the curve had the direction of the 1-year yield right in about
6 months of 10. From January 2009 to May 2011 it implied a rise every
month, and a year later the 1-year yield was lower or unchanged every
time.

A term premium alone would put the actual rate below $f$. In three of
the four months in the table it came in above, so those misses were
surprises about where rates went, not the premium.

## What this means for valuation

Discount a cash flow at the yield that matches its horizon. In July 2023
breaking that rule cut the opposite way from usual. Take a certain \$100
due in ten years:

$$
\frac{100}{1.0397^{10}} = 67.75
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\frac{100}{1.0555^{10}} = 58.27 .
$$

Its present value was \$67.75 at the 10-year yield of 3.97%, and \$58.27
at the 3-month yield of 5.55%. Discounting the long cash flow at the
short rate would have understated its value by 14%. With a normal curve
the same mistake overstates value. In June 2004 it would have given
\$87.62 instead of \$63.66, about 38% too much.

## Takeaways

- An inverted curve implies lower short rates ahead. The forward-rate
  formula turns two yields into that implied rate.
- Since 2000, two of the three inversions were followed by a recession,
  14 and 25 months after they began. The longest one has not been.
- The implied rate had the direction of the 1-year yield right in about
  6 months of 10, and it often missed the size by a wide margin. Treat it
  as the market's price, not as a promise.

## Limits

- The data is month-end. An inversion that lasts a few days inside a
  month does not show: the 10-year yield was below the 2-year yield on
  three days in late August 2019, and the month closed at 0.00.
- Three inversions are a small sample. One more episode could change
  the count from two in three to two in four.
- The forward-rate arithmetic treats published Treasury yields as if the
  bonds paid no coupons.
- The NBER dates recessions after the fact, sometimes a year later, so
  the last months of the data can still change.
